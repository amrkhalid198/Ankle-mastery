Chapter 1Osteology and joint architecture

The foot and ankle are 28 bones and 33 articulations arranged to do two contradictory jobs: absorb and adapt at contact, then convert into a rigid lever at push-off. Almost every pathology in this curriculum is a failure of that conversion, in one direction or the other. Learn the architecture as a mechanism, not as a list.

The talus — the bone with no muscle attachments

The talus has no muscular or tendinous insertions. Every force it experiences is transmitted through articular surfaces and ligaments. Three consequences follow, and you will use all three clinically:

  • It cannot be moved voluntarily. Talar position is dictated by tibial motion above and calcaneal/navicular motion below. This is why "control the talus" is a meaningless cue and why proximal and distal control are the only levers you have.
  • Its blood supply is precarious and largely retrograde, entering distally via the artery of the tarsal canal (posterior tibial), the artery of the tarsal sinus (perforating fibular / dorsalis pedis), and dorsal neck branches. Talar neck fractures are graded by displacement precisely because displacement strips this supply; avascular necrosis risk rises steeply with Hawkins grade.
  • Roughly 60% of its surface is articular cartilage, leaving little area for capsular attachment and healing. This is the anatomical reason osteochondral lesions of the talus (OLTs) heal poorly and persist as "the sprain that never got better."

The trochlea tali — the dome — is a truncated cone, wedge-shaped in the transverse plane: anteriorly wider than posteriorly, by roughly 2.4 mm on average. Hold that number. It generates the single most useful mechanical fact about the ankle:

Load this one first In dorsiflexion, the wide anterior trochlea drives into the mortise. The joint becomes close-packed: maximally congruent, ligaments taut, bony stability high. In plantarflexion, the narrow posterior trochlea sits in the mortise and the joint is loose-packed: less congruent, more accessory motion, stability handed over to ligaments. This is why the ankle sprains in plantarflexion, why the anterior drawer test is performed in slight plantarflexion, and why forced dorsiflexion injures the syndesmosis rather than the ATFL.

The mortise

The talocrural joint is a mortise-and-tenon: the tibial plafond above, medial malleolus medially, lateral malleolus laterally. Note the asymmetry, because it determines injury patterns:

  • The lateral malleolus extends roughly 1 cm more distally than the medial and sits posteriorly. It is a bony block against eversion. There is no equivalent lateral block against inversion — hence the 4:1 lateral-to-medial sprain ratio.
  • The medial malleolus is short, so medial-side restraint is ligamentous (deltoid) rather than bony. A deltoid strong enough to resist eversion means the bone fails first: this is why forced eversion tends to produce a medial malleolar avulsion fracture rather than a deltoid rupture.
  • The fibula bears roughly 10–17% of axial load and, critically, migrates: it externally rotates, translates posteriorly and moves proximally by 1–2 mm during dorsiflexion, accommodating the widening mortise.
Clinical consequence A fibula that cannot migrate — because of syndesmotic scarring, a rigid screw, or capsular restriction — produces a hard dorsiflexion block and anterior ankle impingement pain. When you see restricted dorsiflexion with a firm, early, anterior end-feel after a syndesmotic injury or ORIF, you are looking at a mortise that cannot widen, not a tight calf.

The incisura fibularis: individual bony topography

The distal fibula does not sit against a flat tibia. It sits in the incisura fibularis — the fibular notch — bounded by the anterior and posterior tibial tubercles. Its morphology varies substantially between people, and that variation is a direct determinant of how much rotational restraint is bony and how much is ligamentous.

Incisura morphologyBony restraint to fibular rotation and translationConsequence of AITFL/IOL injury
Deep, concave (crescentic)High — the fibula is seated in a socketLigament injury is partially compensated by bony containment; less rotational instability for the same ligamentous lesion
Shallow, flat (reported in a substantial minority of ankles)Low — restraint is almost entirely ligamentousThe same ligament injury produces greater fibular external rotation and posterior translation; higher risk of persistent instability and of losing reduction after fixation
The imaging trap Syndesmotic instability in a shallow incisura is predominantly rotational and translational in the axial plane, not a widening of the mortise on an AP radiograph. Tibiofibular clear space and overlap can look normal while the fibula is externally rotated and sitting posteriorly in the notch. This is why axial CT — with bilateral comparison — is the assessment of choice: incisura morphology is so individual that the patient's uninjured side is the only valid reference standard. A "normal" AP mortise view does not exclude syndesmotic instability.
Rehabilitation implication Two athletes with identical grade of AITFL injury will have different tolerance for rotational load depending on bony topography you cannot see on examination. This is one of the concrete reasons that syndesmotic rehabilitation is criterion-driven and rotation-tested (single-leg rotational hop, cutting tolerance) rather than run on a fixed timeline. Volume 4 builds the criteria.

The subtalar joint

Talus on calcaneus, across three facets: posterior (the largest, a separate synovial cavity), and middle and anterior (which share a cavity with the talonavicular joint — together forming the talocalcaneonavicular complex). The posterior and middle facets are separated by the tarsal canal, which flares laterally into the sinus tarsi. Inside sit the interosseous talocalcaneal ligament and the cervical ligament, plus a dense population of mechanoreceptors.

The subtalar screw: how supination generates a bony clamp

The three facets do not share a curvature. They are inverted relative to one another, and this is the entire mechanism:

  • Posterior facet — convex on the calcaneus, concave on the talus.
  • Anterior and middle facets — concave on the calcaneus, convex on the talus.

A single joint, one oblique axis, two oppositely curved surface pairs. Pure rotation is geometrically impossible: the talus must translate while it rotates. Manter (1941) characterised the resulting path as a helix — a right-handed screw in the right foot, left-handed in the left — with the talus advancing along the axis as it rotates about it.

Run the mechanism in supination. The posterior facet drives into congruency while the anterior and middle facets simultaneously close in the opposite direction; the interosseous talocalcaneal and cervical ligaments wind and shorten; the tarsal canal narrows. Surface congruency and ligamentous winding rise together, and subtalar stiffness climbs steeply toward end-range. In pronation the surfaces unwind, congruency falls, and the joint becomes loose-packed and compliant.

This is the hindfoot half of the rigid lever Most clinicians attribute push-off rigidity entirely to the windlass. The windlass is the forefoot and plantar contribution. The subtalar screw is the hindfoot contribution, and it is the one that comes first in the sequence — calcaneal inversion begins in mid-to-late stance, before hallux dorsiflexion engages the fascia. A foot that cannot supinate at the hindfoot cannot be rescued by an intact windlass, because the proximal segment of the lever is still compliant.
Precision correction The screw analogy is a description of a path, not a locking device. There is no discrete "lock" — stiffness rises progressively as congruency and ligament tension increase, and the effective pitch of the helix varies substantially between individuals because it is determined by subtalar axis orientation, which itself ranges from roughly 20° to 68° of inclination. Do not tell patients or students that the subtalar joint "locks." State that it stiffens, and that how much it stiffens per degree is individual.

Clinical consequence Hindfoot supination can be lost three ways, and they need different treatment: structural (talocalcaneal coalition, post-traumatic arthrofibrosis of the posterior facet, subtalar arthritis), positional (a hindfoot held in eversion by tibialis posterior insufficiency or attritional failure of the spring ligament), and motor (inhibited tibialis posterior and fibularis longus after injury). Push-off looks equally weak in all three. Passive subtalar inversion range in prone, non-weight-bearing, separates the first from the other two immediately.
Why sinus tarsi syndrome exists The sinus tarsi is opened by supination and closed by pronation. Post-sprain, it commonly fills with scar and synovitis. The presenting complaint — lateral ankle pain, worse on uneven ground, with a sense of instability out of proportion to laxity testing — is often mechanoreceptor-rich tissue that is irritated and giving degraded afferent information, not a lax ATFL. You will meet this repeatedly in Volume 2.

The inferior extensor retinaculum is not a strap

The IER is usually taught as a Y-shaped pulley holding the anterior tendons down. That description omits the part that matters. Its stem sends three deep roots into the sinus tarsi and tarsal canal — conventionally the lateral (oblique), intermediate and medial roots, described collectively as the frondiform ligament. These roots blend with the interosseous talocalcaneal and cervical ligaments and anchor into the floor of the sinus.

Three functions follow, and only the first is usually taught:

  1. Pulley — retains tibialis anterior, EHL and EDL against the dorsum during dorsiflexion.
  2. Passive restraint — the roots contribute to subtalar and talocrural stability by tensioning across the tarsal canal.
  3. Afferent anchor — the roots occupy the most mechanoreceptor-dense region of the hindfoot and are tensioned by subtalar motion, positioning them to report hindfoot position continuously.

The surgical detail that proves the point In the Gould modification of the Broström repair, the surgeon mobilises the IER and sutures it over the imbricated ATFL. This is conventionally justified as mechanical reinforcement — but it also restores tension to a densely innervated structure and reconstructs the sinus tarsi anchorage. It is a plausible part of why the Broström-Gould outperforms simple ligament imbrication on functional, not just laxity, outcomes.
Evidence status The anatomy of the three roots is well established and the mechanoreceptor density of the sinus tarsi region is documented. The specific proprioceptive contribution of the IER roots has not been quantified in vivo — it is an anatomically grounded inference, not a measured function. Use it to explain why sinus tarsi scarring produces symptoms disproportionate to laxity; do not present it as demonstrated fact.

The transverse tarsal (Chopart) joint

Talonavicular plus calcaneocuboid — the anatomical hinge between hindfoot and midfoot, and the classic explanation for how the foot switches between flexible and rigid.

Contested — do not teach this as settled The traditional model (Elftman, 1960) holds that in hindfoot pronation the talonavicular and calcaneocuboid axes become parallel, unlocking the midfoot; in supination they diverge, locking it. This is taught almost universally. It has not held up well: Blackwood et al. (2005) measured midfoot stiffness directly in cadavers and found supination increased stiffness, but the magnitude was not explained by axis alignment alone, and subsequent in-vivo bone-pin work (Lundgren et al., 2008) shows far more midfoot motion during gait than the model predicts. Use the functional observation — a supinated hindfoot yields a stiffer midfoot — and drop the axis-parallelism explanation. The stiffening is multifactorial: plantar fascia tension, ligamentous winding, joint congruency, and intrinsic muscle activity.

What to use instead: Manter's two axes

Manter (1941) described midtarsal motion about two distinct axes rather than a single locking mechanism. This model is descriptively accurate, clinically usable, and does not depend on the discredited parallelism argument.

AxisInclination from transverse planeDeviation from sagittal planeMotion produced
Longitudinal~15°~9°Almost pure frontal-plane motion — forefoot inversion/eversion on the hindfoot
Oblique~52°~57°Coupled sagittal and transverse motion — dorsiflexion with abduction, plantarflexion with adduction
Turn two axes into a diagnosis The axes let you name what a midfoot is doing instead of calling everything "pronation."

Longitudinal-axis problem — the forefoot is compensating in the frontal plane. Presentation: forefoot varus or supinatus, forefoot inverted relative to the hindfoot on non-weight-bearing examination, hindfoot everting under load to bring the medial forefoot to the ground. Intervention targets frontal-plane load distribution: forefoot posting, first-ray control, fibularis longus.

Oblique-axis problem — the midfoot is absorbing sagittal-plane demand the ankle cannot supply. Presentation: the "midfoot break," an early and excessive dorsiflexion/abduction through the midfoot in late stance, almost always with restricted talocrural dorsiflexion. Intervention targets the ankle first (posterior talar glide, dorsiflexion capacity), because the midfoot is the symptom, not the lesion.

Same-looking flat foot in gait, opposite treatment.

Tarsometatarsal (Lisfranc) complex

Three columns: medial (1st TMT, mobile), middle (2nd and 3rd, essentially rigid), lateral (4th and 5th, mobile, ~10–20° sagittal motion to accommodate uneven ground).

The second metatarsal base is recessed into a mortise between the medial and lateral cuneiforms — the keystone of the arch. There is no intermetatarsal ligament between the 1st and 2nd metatarsal bases. Instead, the Lisfranc ligament runs obliquely from the lateral aspect of the medial cuneiform to the plantar-medial base of the 2nd metatarsal, with interosseous, plantar and dorsal bands; the plantar band is the strongest.

Missed-injury trap Because there is no 1st–2nd intermetatarsal ligament, Lisfranc injury shows as widening of the 1st–2nd interspace — and only on weight-bearing or stress views. Non-weight-bearing radiographs are normal in a substantial proportion of subtle Lisfranc injuries. A midfoot injury with plantar ecchymosis and pain on the 2nd TMT that is "cleared" by a non-weight-bearing X-ray is one of the highest-consequence misses in the whole foot. Volume 5 covers this in full.

First ray and sesamoids

The first ray is the 1st metatarsal plus medial cuneiform, moving as a functional unit. It carries roughly twice the load of any lesser ray at push-off. The two sesamoids sit within the flexor hallucis brevis tendons in the plantar plate, elevating the 1st metatarsal head and increasing the moment arm of hallux flexion.

The first ray axis is triplanar, and the coupling is obligatory

Hicks (1953) established that the first ray does not move in the sagittal plane. Its axis runs obliquely, inclined at approximately 45°, passing from dorsal-medial-proximal to plantar-lateral-distal. Because the axis is oblique, motion about it is triplanar and the coupling is mechanically obligatory, not a choice:

First ray motionObligatory coupled motionFunctional context
DorsiflexionInversion (and slight abduction)Loading response — the first ray gives way and rolls into inversion as load arrives
PlantarflexionEversion (and slight adduction)Late stance — the 1st metatarsal head must plantarflex and evert to be pressed flat into the ground
Why fibularis longus is irreplaceable For the first metatarsal head to be driven flat into the ground at push-off, the first ray must plantarflex and evert simultaneously. Fibularis longus is the only muscle that produces both components — it approaches the first ray from the plantar-lateral side through the cuboid groove, so its line of pull matches the 45° axis almost exactly. Flexor hallucis brevis and the intrinsics can plantarflex; none of them can evert the ray. This is why fibularis longus weakness cannot be substituted for, and why "first ray strengthening" that consists of toe flexion drills does not restore late-stance first-ray function.
The cascade to memorise Fibularis longus inhibition after a lateral ankle sprain → the first ray is not plantarflexed and everted in late stance → it sits relatively dorsiflexed and inverted → the 1st metatarsal head does not accept load → load transfers to the 2nd metatarsal (2nd MT stress injury, transfer metatarsalgia) → and because a dorsiflexed first ray raises the MTP axis, hallux dorsiflexion is mechanically obstructed under load → functional hallux limitus → the windlass is not triggered → sustained plantar fascia strain. One inhibited evertor, three separate downstream diagnoses. Test fibularis longus by resisted first-ray plantarflexion, not by generic eversion, which fibularis brevis will mask.

Self-test — Chapter 1

  1. A patient has full plantarflexion but 5° of dorsiflexion with a hard, early anterior end-feel three months after ankle ORIF. Name two structural explanations and say how you would distinguish them.
    (a) The fibula cannot migrate/externally rotate — syndesmotic scarring or retained hardware limiting mortise widening; (b) anterior tibiotalar osteophyte or capsular impingement. Distinguish: a bony block is a hard, abrupt end-feel unchanged by knee position and unchanged by non-weight-bearing vs weight-bearing testing; a soft-tissue calf restriction changes with knee flexion (gastrocnemius) and gives a springy end-feel. Palpable anterior joint-line tenderness and pain reproduced at end-range dorsiflexion favour impingement; a fibular migration problem is often painless but rigid.
  2. Why does the talus have no muscle attachments, and what does that mean for treatment?
    It is almost entirely articular surface (~60%) and functions purely as a force-transmitting intercalated segment. Clinically: you cannot train the talus. Talar position is a consequence of tibial and calcaneal/navicular control, so all intervention is directed above and below it. It also explains retrograde vascular supply and poor healing of talar dome lesions.
  3. State the trochlear width difference and give three clinical facts that follow from it.
    ~2.4 mm wider anteriorly. (1) Dorsiflexion is the close-packed position — high bony stability, so ankles rarely sprain in dorsiflexion; (2) plantarflexion is loose-packed — ligamentous stability dominates, which is when the lateral complex fails; (3) anterior drawer testing must be done in ~10–20° plantarflexion, otherwise bony congruency masks laxity.

Chapter 2Ligaments: the restraint system

Ligaments are not passive straps. They are load-bearing, viscoelastic, richly innervated sensory organs. Every ligament in this chapter has a mechanical role and an afferent role, and injury degrades both. When you finish Volume 2 you will understand that most of the long-term disability after ankle sprain comes from the second one.

Deltoid (medial) complex

A two-layer fan, and the layers do different jobs.

  • Superficial — tibionavicular, tibiospring, tibiocalcaneal, superficial posterior tibiotalar. Crosses talocrural and subtalar. Primary restraint to hindfoot eversion.
  • Deep — anterior and posterior tibiotalar. Short, thick, intra-articular. Primary restraint to lateral talar translation and external rotation of the talus in the mortise.

The deep deltoid is the structure that matters in fracture decision-making: if it is intact, the talus cannot shift laterally, and an isolated distal fibular fracture can often be treated non-operatively. If it is torn, the ankle is unstable and usually surgical.

Syndesmosis

Four structures bind the distal tibia and fibula: AITFL, PITFL (with its deep component, the transverse tibiofibular ligament), the interosseous ligament (IOL), and the interosseous membrane. Relative contribution to resisting diastasis (Ogilvie-Harris et al., 1994):

StructureContribution to syndesmotic stability
PITFL (incl. transverse)~42%
AITFL~35%
IOL~22%

Injury mechanism is external rotation of the talus in the mortise, typically with the foot planted and dorsiflexed — the wide anterior trochlea acts as a wedge forcing the fibula away from the tibia. This is why the syndesmotic injury is a dorsiflexion injury while the lateral sprain is a plantarflexion injury, and why they behave completely differently in rehab (Volume 4).

Reframe screw removal Screw removal is often described to patients as "taking the metal out." Mechanically it is restoring the fibula's ability to migrate, which restores the mortise's ability to widen, which restores end-range dorsiflexion and its coupled external rotation. Screw breakage is frequently asymptomatic or symptom-relieving for exactly this reason: the construct has failed in a way that returns motion to the joint. Reduction accuracy — not construct type alone — remains the dominant determinant of long-term outcome; a malreduced suture-button is worse than a well-reduced screw.

Bassett's ligament — a normal structure that becomes pathological

The AITFL is not a single band. Its most distal fibres form a discrete accessory fascicle, separated from the main ligament body by a fibrofatty septum and running more obliquely — described by Bassett et al. (1990) and now carrying his name. It is present in the majority of ankles and is a normal anatomical variant, not a lesion. It lies directly against the anterolateral corner of the talar dome.

The pathology is secondary. After ATFL insufficiency, the talus sits and translates anteriorly and rotates internally within the mortise. The anterolateral talar dome is now presented against the distal fascicle. During dorsiflexion the fascicle is dragged across articular cartilage it never previously contacted, producing abrasion, synovitis, and eventually a chondral erosion at the anterolateral corner.

A leading answer to "the sprain that never got better" Presentation: persistent anterolateral ankle pain months after a lateral sprain, sharp and reproduced at end-range dorsiflexion (not plantarflexion), tenderness localised to the anterolateral gutter, sometimes with a sense of catching. Laxity testing may be unremarkable. MRI is frequently reported as normal because the fascicle itself is normal tissue; the diagnosis is clinical and confirmed arthroscopically, where resection is reliably effective.

Put it in your differential alongside the other three persistent-lateral-pain diagnoses: osteochondral lesion of the talus (deep, load-related joint-line pain, effusion, catching), sinus tarsi syndrome (pain on uneven ground, instability out of proportion to laxity, closed by pronation), and superficial fibular nerve traction injury (dorsal foot burning, positive Tinel's above the lateral malleolus). Four different pathologies, one presenting complaint. Volume 2 gives you the sequence that separates them in a single examination.
Number worth memorising A 1 mm lateral shift of the talus reduces tibiotalar contact area by roughly 40%, concentrating contact stress. This is the mechanical basis of post-traumatic ankle osteoarthritis, and the reason syndesmotic and deltoid injuries are treated far more aggressively than lateral sprains despite being less common.

The medial arch restraints

Static support of the medial longitudinal arch is a layered system. Huang et al. (1993) sectioned structures sequentially in cadaver feet and measured the loss of arch stiffness:

StructureContribution to arch stiffness
Plantar aponeurosis~25%
Long plantar ligament~17%
Plantar calcaneonavicular (spring) ligament~10%

The spring ligament complex (superomedial and inferoplantar longitudinal bands) forms a sling under the talar head. It is the structure that fails alongside tibialis posterior in progressive collapsing foot deformity — and the reason PCFD is not simply "a weak tib post."

Order of operations Static restraints do not fail first in a vacuum. In PCFD the sequence is: tibialis posterior tendon dysfunction → increased load on spring ligament and deltoid → attritional failure of those static restraints → deformity becomes fixed. Once the static restraints have failed, strengthening the tendon will not restore the arch. Staging (Volume 4) is therefore a decision about which structures are still intact, not about how much pain there is.

Plantar fascia

Not really a ligament, not really a fascia: a thick aponeurosis from the medial calcaneal tuberosity, dividing into five bands inserting into the plantar plates and proximal phalanges. Because it inserts distal to the MTP joints, it functions as a cable around a drum — the windlass (Chapter 6).

The calcaneal bridge: Achilles–plantar fascia continuity

This chapter has described the plantar aponeurosis as an arch restraint and Chapter 3 will describe the Achilles as a plantarflexor. That separation is an artefact of dissection technique, not of anatomy. The two structures are mechanically continuous across the calcaneus, and the continuity is the reason a large part of plantar heel pain is treated at the calf.

The continuity has three components, and they are worth holding separately because they behave differently:

  1. Paratenon into periosteum. The paratenon investing the Achilles does not terminate at the tendon's insertion. It continues as the periosteum of the posterior and plantar calcaneus and blends with the fascial envelope of the plantar aponeurosis at its origin on the medial calcaneal tuberosity (Stecco et al., 2013). There is no anatomical point at which one structure ends and the other begins; there is a region in which the composition changes.
  2. Direct fibrous continuity, age-dependent. Snow et al. (1995) sectioned calcanei across age bands and found fibrous continuity between the Achilles insertion and the plantar fascia origin in every specimen, but with a clear gradient: a thick, unambiguous periosteal sheet in the young, progressively thinner with each decade. The bridge does not disappear with age — it becomes thin. Note the clinical inversion this predicts and the epidemiology confirms: the adolescent with an open apophysis presents with calcaneal apophysitis (Chapter 7), where the traction of both structures converges on the growth plate; the middle-aged adult, with the same tension but a thinner bridge and a stiffer calf, presents with plantar heel pain.
  3. Trabecular continuity through the bone. The calcaneus is not an inert spacer between two soft tissues. Its cancellous architecture is organised into named tensile and compressive trabecular systems that run between the posterior tuberosity, the plantar surface and the posterior facet, leaving a low-density neutral triangle between them. Load entering at the Achilles insertion is transmitted through that lattice toward the plantar origin rather than being absorbed by the bone. This is also why the neutral triangle is the low-resistance region in which calcaneal stress fractures and compressive stress reactions appear.
Two tensioning routes, only one of which is the windlass The plantar aponeurosis can be loaded by two mechanically independent routes.

Route 1 — distal (the windlass). MTP dorsiflexion winds the aponeurosis around the metatarsal heads. Requires an available hallux. Covered in Chapter 6.

Route 2 — proximal (the calcaneal bridge). Ankle dorsiflexion lengthens the triceps surae–Achilles unit; that tension is transmitted across the calcaneus and strains the plantar fascia with the MTP joints in neutral and the windlass entirely disengaged. Cadaveric loading confirms the coupling directly: raising Achilles tension raises plantar fascia strain in near-proportion (Carlson et al., 2000), and finite-element modelling of the standing foot identifies Achilles force as the single largest contributor to plantar fascia tension, larger than body weight itself (Cheung et al., 2006). For scale: buckle-transducer measurement during walking puts peak plantar aponeurosis tension at roughly 96% of body weight in late stance (Erdemir et al., 2004) — and late stance is precisely when Achilles tension and MTP dorsiflexion are both maximal, so the two routes load the same tissue simultaneously.
What this changes in practice 1. Dorsiflexion restriction is a plantar fascia problem, not just an ankle problem. In a matched case-control study, ankle dorsiflexion of 0° or less carried an odds ratio of roughly 23 for plantar fasciitis compared with more than 10° (Riddle et al., 2003) — the strongest single risk factor identified in that study. Route 2 explains the number: a restricted ankle means the fascia is tensioned through the bridge on every step of midstance, before the windlass has even engaged.

2. Heavy calf loading is a plantar fascia treatment, not an adjunct. Rathleff et al. (2015) randomised patients with plantar fasciitis to plantar-specific stretching or to high-load progressive strength training — slow single-leg heel raises performed with a towel rolled under the toes — and found superior Foot Function Index scores in the strength group at 3 months, converging by 6 and 12. Read the exercise mechanically: the towel pre-tensions Route 1 while the heel raise loads Route 2, so the aponeurosis is loaded through both of its tensioning pathways at once. That is not a stretching protocol with a load added; it is a deliberate loading of the whole calcaneal bridge.

3. It explains the pairings you keep seeing. Insertional Achilles pain and plantar heel pain in the same patient; a night splint helping both; an ipsilateral calf that is stiff on the symptomatic heel side; plantar heel pain that emerges after a period of forced plantarflexion — post-immobilisation, or a sudden change to a heeled shoe — because the bridge is stiff and short at exactly the point where it is loaded first.
Evidence status — the local link is solid, the long chain is a model The Achilles–calcaneus–plantar fascia link is supported by dissection, cadaveric loading and modelling. It is safe to reason with and safe to treat from.

The Superficial Back Line — the wider myofascial chain running plantar fascia → Achilles → hamstrings → sacrotuberous ligament → erector spinae → epicranial fascia — is a different order of claim. A systematic review of dissection evidence for myofascial continuity found the continuity itself reasonably well supported for several transitions along this chain, while noting that studies were largely descriptive and heterogeneous (Wilke et al., 2016). Anatomical continuity is not force transmission. Remote stretching effects (hamstring stretching increasing ankle range, and the reverse) are reported but small, and a neural mediator explains them at least as well as a mechanical one. Use the two-segment calcaneal bridge as mechanism; use the full line as a heuristic for where to look, never as an explanation you give a patient. And do not invert the logic into a release rationale: cutting the plantar fascia does relieve its tension, at the cost of measurable arch collapse and lateral column overload.

Self-test — Chapter 2

  1. An athlete has a positive talar tilt test. What can you infer about the ATFL, and why?
    The ATFL is almost certainly torn. Talar tilt principally tests the CFL, and the lateral complex fails sequentially ATFL → CFL → PTFL. CFL insufficiency without ATFL injury would be mechanically anomalous.
  2. Why does a syndesmotic injury present with pain on forced dorsiflexion, while a lateral sprain does not?
    Dorsiflexion drives the wide anterior trochlea into the mortise and forces the tibia and fibula apart, loading the injured syndesmotic ligaments. In a lateral sprain the ATFL is slack in dorsiflexion, so dorsiflexion is comparatively comfortable; the ATFL is loaded in plantarflexion and inversion.
  3. A patient with a fibular fracture has medial-sided tenderness and swelling. Why does that single finding change management?
    It raises suspicion of deep deltoid disruption. With the deep deltoid torn, the talus can translate laterally in the mortise; 1 mm of lateral shift cuts tibiotalar contact area by ~40%. That converts a stable, non-operative fibular fracture into an unstable, usually operative injury.
  4. Rank the three main contributors to static medial arch stiffness.
    Plantar aponeurosis (~25%) > long plantar ligament (~17%) > spring ligament (~10%) (Huang et al., 1993).

Chapter 3Muscles, tendons and moment arms

A muscle's clinical relevance is force capacity × moment arm × the plane it acts in. Learn the ankle musculature that way and prescription becomes obvious.

Posterior compartment — the engine

Triceps surae (gastrocnemius medial and lateral heads, soleus) into the Achilles. This group produces the largest joint moment in human locomotion. Key facts:

  • Gastrocnemius is biarticular (crosses the knee), soleus is monoarticular. Therefore knee position is the single test that separates them, in assessment (weight-bearing lunge with knee straight vs bent) and in loading (straight-knee vs bent-knee calf raise).
  • Soleus is predominantly type I, has a large physiological cross-sectional area, and contributes the majority of plantarflexion force during stance. It is the workhorse and it is almost always the undertrained one.
  • The Achilles has a plantarflexion moment arm of roughly 4–6 cm, varying with ankle angle and between individuals. A larger moment arm means less tendon force is needed for the same joint moment — but also less tendon stretch and less elastic energy storage. This is a genuine trade-off, not a "better/worse" trait.
  • Achilles fibres spiral roughly 90° from origin to insertion and are arranged in sub-tendons (from medial gastroc, lateral gastroc, soleus) with limited sliding between them. Non-uniform displacement between sub-tendons is a candidate mechanism for regional tendinopathy — plausible, not proven.

The catapult: calf fascicles do not lengthen and shorten during running

The standard description — plantarflexors work eccentrically through midstance, then concentrically at push-off — describes the muscle–tendon unit, not the muscle. Ultrasound measurement of fascicle behaviour during locomotion (Lichtwark & Wilson, 2006; Ishikawa et al., 2007) shows something different at the fibre level:

  • Gastrocnemius and soleus fascicles operate quasi-isometrically during stance — near-constant length, high force.
  • Essentially all of the muscle–tendon unit's length change is taken up by the tendon and aponeurosis, which stretch during energy absorption and recoil during propulsion.
  • The muscle is functioning as an anchor and force generator; the tendon is the spring. Energy is loaded into the tendon and released faster than muscle could produce it — a catapult, not a motor.

Two mechanical advantages follow. Isometric contraction sits at the high-force region of the force–velocity relationship, so force capacity is maximised. And isometric force production is metabolically cheap relative to shortening contraction, so the metabolic cost of running is far lower than muscle-driven propulsion would require.

What goes wrong when tendon stiffness falls Tendinopathy, prolonged immobilisation, and post-repair tendon elongation all reduce tendon stiffness (increase compliance). A more compliant tendon absorbs a larger share of the MTU length change only up to a point — beyond it, the tendon can no longer hold the muscle at its operating length under load, and the fascicles are forced to lengthen and then shorten to complete the movement. Consequences: the muscle leaves the isometric high-force region and drops onto a worse part of the force–velocity curve; metabolic cost per step rises; and fatigue arrives early.

The clinical picture this explains: the runner with Achilles tendinopathy who says the calf feels "dead," "burnt out," or "heavy" within a few kilometres, whose calf endurance testing is not obviously poor, and whose complaint is fatigue rather than pain. That is plausibly a tendon stiffness problem presenting as a muscle endurance problem. It also reframes what heavy slow resistance training is doing: increasing tendon stiffness and restoring the catapult is arguably the primary mechanism, not "healing" the tendon.

Inter-fascicular sliding and the interfascicular matrix

The Achilles is not one cable. It is three subtendons — from medial gastrocnemius, lateral gastrocnemius and soleus — that spiral roughly 90° from origin to insertion. Speckle-tracking ultrasound shows they displace non-uniformly during loading, with the deep (soleus) portion displacing substantially more than the superficial gastrocnemius portions during plantarflexion (Slane & Thelen, 2014).

This independent gliding is permitted by the interfascicular matrix (IFM) — the elastin- and lubricin-rich compartment between fascicles. In energy-storing tendons the IFM is the compliant, extensible, fatigue-resistant element: it allows fascicles to slide past one another, recover, and distribute load, and it contributes disproportionately to whole-tendon extensibility (Thorpe et al., 2015).

The failure mechanism follows directly. With ageing, metabolic load and accumulated cycles, non-enzymatic glycation deposits advanced glycation end-products that cross-link the IFM. The matrix stiffens, sliding falls, and the subtendons become mechanically coupled. Differential displacement that was previously accommodated by sliding is now converted into localised interfascicular shear strain — concentrated where the twist is greatest and the subtendons are most obliquely arranged: the mid-portion, 2–6 cm above the insertion.

Evidence status — important Non-uniform subtendon displacement, IFM composition and function, and glycation-driven IFM stiffening are all demonstrated. The link — that loss of inter-fascicular sliding is the causal driver of mid-portion Achilles tendinopathy — is the leading mechanical hypothesis and explains the site, the age association and the load association better than the discarded "watershed hypovascularity" model. It has not been demonstrated causally in vivo in humans. Reason with it; do not tell patients their tendon is "glycated" or "stuck together." What it does justify clinically is loading that includes varied ankle and knee positions and rotational components, on the reasoning that uniform straight-plane loading trains one subtendon distribution and may leave the shear interfaces untrained.

Deep posterior compartment: tibialis posterior, flexor digitorum longus, flexor hallucis longus. Tib post is the primary invertor and the dynamic supporter of the medial arch; it has a short excursion, so small losses of length or strength produce large functional consequences. FHL runs beneath the sustentaculum tali and through a fibro-osseous tunnel — the site of posterior ankle pain in dancers and the reason FHL tenosynovitis mimics posterior impingement.

Lateral compartment — the guardians

  • Fibularis brevis: inserts on the 5th metatarsal base; the strongest pure evertor. Its insertion explains the avulsion fracture at the 5th MT base in an inversion injury.
  • Fibularis longus: crosses the plantar foot in the cuboid groove to insert on the 1st metatarsal base and medial cuneiform. It plantarflexes and everts the first ray, pressing the first metatarsal head into the ground. This makes it essential for first-ray stability and for the windlass to work — an under-recognised link between lateral ankle injury and later forefoot and plantar heel problems.

Anterior compartment

Tibialis anterior, extensor hallucis longus, extensor digitorum longus, fibularis tertius. Their job is not just swing-phase clearance — it is eccentric control of foot lowering from initial contact to foot-flat. Tib ant weakness shows as foot slap, and as the "controlled heel strike" that disappears when a runner fatigues.

Intrinsic foot muscles — the foot core

Four plantar layers plus extensor digitorum brevis. Their role was long dismissed. Current evidence indicates they act as active stabilisers of the arch: Kelly et al. (2014) showed that electrically stimulating abductor hallucis, flexor digitorum brevis and quadratus plantae reduced arch deformation and navicular drop under load. McKeon et al. (2015) reframed this as the "foot core" system — passive (bone/ligament), active (intrinsics), neural (plantar afferents).

Evidence status The mechanical role of the intrinsics in arch support is well supported. What is not established is that intrinsic strengthening ("short foot" exercise, toe yoga) meaningfully changes clinical outcomes in plantar heel pain, PCFD or injury prevention. Trials are small, short and heterogeneous, with inconsistent effects on pain and function. Prescribe it as a reasonable, low-cost adjunct — not as a proven treatment. Do not build a program around it.

Self-test — Chapter 3

  1. A runner has full dorsiflexion with the knee bent but 5° with the knee straight. Which muscle, and what does that change in your program?
    Gastrocnemius — it is biarticular, so straight-knee testing lengthens it while bent-knee testing slackens it. Program: bias straight-knee loading and lengthening (straight-knee calf raise, straight-knee calf stretch/loaded lengthening); soleus capacity is likely adequate but should still be tested for endurance, not just range.
  2. Why does a lateral ankle sprain sometimes lead to first-ray and plantar heel problems months later?
    Fibularis longus plantarflexes and everts the first ray, pressing the 1st metatarsal head into the ground. If it is inhibited or weak after injury, the first ray is less stable, hallux dorsiflexion at push-off is compromised, and the windlass mechanism becomes less effective — increasing plantar fascia strain and altering forefoot load distribution.
  3. Which plantarflexor produces most of the force during stance, and why is it commonly undertrained?
    Soleus — large physiological cross-sectional area, predominantly type I, and it is not shortened by knee flexion. It is undertrained because most calf work is done with a straight knee and low volume, which biases gastrocnemius and never reaches soleus's endurance and high-load requirements (running demands are on the order of several times body weight, repeatedly).

Chapter 4Joint axes and arthrokinematics

This is the chapter most clinicians skip and the one that separates a specialist from a technician. Foot and ankle motion is triplanar because the axes are oblique. If you can state the axes, you can predict every coupled motion in the chain without memorising a single one.

The talocrural axis is not fixed

The malleolar axis given below is an approximation that holds across mid-range and fails at the ends. The reason is geometric. The trochlea tali is not a cylinder: it approximates a section of a truncated cone with its apex directed medially. Therefore the lateral margin has a larger radius of curvature — and a correspondingly longer arc — than the medial margin.

The consequence is unavoidable. For the talus to rotate through a given sagittal angle, the lateral side must travel further than the medial side. Since both are constrained within the same mortise, the difference is expressed as yaw: the talus externally rotates and abducts as it dorsiflexes, and internally rotates and adducts as it plantarflexes.

Reframe the coupling The dorsiflexion–abduction–eversion coupling is not produced by muscles, ligaments or the subtalar joint. It is an obligatory output of asymmetric trochlear geometry. Nothing in the soft tissue can abolish it. This matters because clinicians routinely try to "correct" the eversion drift they see during dorsiflexion assessment or during a lunge, treating it as a fault. Within its physiological magnitude, it is the joint working correctly.

Because the arcs differ, the instantaneous centre of rotation (ICR) migrates through range rather than sitting on a fixed hinge (Barnett & Napier, 1952; Lundberg et al., 1989). The functional axis is directed downward and laterally in plantarflexion and shifts to run upward and laterally as the ankle moves into dorsiflexion. The ankle is a sliding, rotating, migrating-axis joint, not a hinge.

Three things a migrating ICR changes in practice 1. Mobilisation direction must change with joint angle. A posterior talar glide applied in one fixed direction is correctly aligned at only one point in range. Reassess and reorient your force as range improves — this is the single most common reason a mobilisation "stops working" after two sessions.

2. Rigid external devices over-restrict at the ends of range. Braces, taping constructs and rigid orthoses are designed against a fixed-hinge assumption. They approximate well mid-range and bind at end-range, which is exactly where the athlete needs the coupled rotation. This is a mechanical explanation for why heavily braced athletes report stiffness at deep dorsiflexion despite adequate measured range.

3. Altered trochlear geometry permanently alters coupling. After an osteochondral lesion of the talar dome, a malunited fracture, or a fibular malunion, the arcs are no longer the ones the mortise was built around. Restoring the angle of dorsiflexion does not restore the path. Expect residual altered kinematics and plan capacity accordingly rather than chasing symmetrical range.

The talocrural axis

Approximated by a line through the tips of the two malleoli. It is not horizontal and not in the frontal plane:

  • Inclined roughly 8–10° from horizontal in the frontal plane (lateral malleolus is more distal).
  • Rotated roughly 20–23° externally from the frontal plane in the transverse plane.

Consequence — the coupling you will use daily: dorsiflexion is accompanied by slight abduction and eversion; plantarflexion by slight adduction and inversion. Pure sagittal ankle motion does not exist.

Why this matters in assessment When you measure dorsiflexion, the foot will drift into abduction and eversion. If you do not block it, you will measure midfoot pronation and call it ankle dorsiflexion — the single commonest measurement error at the ankle. Either control the calcaneus and midfoot in non-weight-bearing testing, or use the weight-bearing lunge test, which is more reliable (ICC typically >0.90 intra-rater) and functionally relevant. Normative knee-to-wall distance is roughly 9–12 cm; asymmetry >1.5 cm is the more meaningful signal.

The subtalar axis

A single oblique axis running posterior-lateral-plantar to anterior-medial-dorsal (Manter, 1941; Inman, 1976):

  • Inclined ~42° from the transverse plane.
  • Deviated ~16–23° medially from the sagittal midline of the foot.
  • Enormous inter-individual variation: reported inclination ranges from roughly 20° to 68°.
Turn the axis into a prediction The orientation of the axis determines how motion is distributed across planes. A high (more vertical) axis converts subtalar motion mostly into transverse-plane motion — more rotation transmitted up the leg per degree of pronation. A low (more horizontal) axis produces mostly frontal-plane motion — more visible calcaneal eversion, less tibial rotation. Two runners with identical rearfoot eversion angles can therefore deliver very different rotational loads to the knee. This is one concrete reason why "overpronation" as a global risk factor has performed so poorly in prospective studies.

Self-test — Chapter 4

  1. State the two angular components of the talocrural axis and the motion coupling that follows.
    ~8–10° from horizontal in the frontal plane; ~20–23° externally rotated from the frontal plane in the transverse plane. Coupling: dorsiflexion → abduction + eversion; plantarflexion → adduction + inversion.
  2. Two athletes have identical calcaneal eversion on video. Why might one have far more knee rotational load?
    Subtalar axis orientation differs. A more vertically inclined axis converts a given amount of pronation into more transverse-plane (tibial rotation) motion; a more horizontal axis yields mostly frontal-plane calcaneal motion. Equal eversion, unequal rotation transmitted proximally.
  3. A basketball player shows knee valgus on landing with 6 cm knee-to-wall bilaterally. What is your first hypothesis and why?
    Restricted dorsiflexion (6 cm is well below the 9–12 cm normative band). Landing requires the tibia to advance over the foot; if the talocrural joint cannot deliver that range, the limb obtains it through midfoot pronation, tibial internal rotation and hip adduction — presenting as dynamic valgus. Treat the ankle before attributing the valgus to hip weakness, but test both.

Mechanoreceptors and afferent supply

Ligaments and capsule contain Ruffini endings, Pacinian corpuscles, Golgi-like endings and free nerve endings. The lateral ligament complex and sinus tarsi are particularly densely innervated. The plantar surface carries four cutaneous receptor types (Meissner, Merkel, Pacinian, Ruffini) whose density is highest at the heel and metatarsal heads, and which encode centre-of-pressure position in real time.

The concept that will run through the whole curriculum An ankle sprain is a partial deafferentation injury, not only a mechanical one. Tearing the ATFL destroys mechanoreceptors along with collagen. The consequence — degraded proprioceptive input, altered postural control, altered feedforward muscle activation — persists after the ligament has healed and is present bilaterally, indicating central rather than purely local reorganisation. This is why balance and sensorimotor training is not an optional extra in ankle rehabilitation; it is the treatment for the impairment that actually predicts recurrence.

Arthrogenic muscle inhibition (AMI)

Joint injury, effusion and altered afferent discharge produce reflex inhibition of muscles crossing the joint — reduced voluntary activation independent of pain and independent of tissue damage. At the ankle this manifests as reduced fibularis and soleus activation, and as reduced corticospinal excitability. Practically: a patient can be pain-free with a normal-looking ligament and still be unable to fully recruit the muscles that protect the joint. Strength testing without measuring activation quality will miss this entirely.

Supraspinal reorganisation: chronic ankle instability is a brain problem

Arthrogenic inhibition is a spinal-level account. It is incomplete. Ligamentous injury produces measurable, persistent reorganisation at cortical and corticospinal levels, and this is where the recurrence risk actually lives (Needle et al., 2017).

Corticospinal suppression

Transcranial magnetic stimulation studies in chronic ankle instability show reduced corticomotor excitability of the muscles that protect the joint: elevated active motor thresholds and reduced motor evoked potential amplitudes for fibularis longus (Pietrosimone & Gribble, 2012) and soleus (Terada et al., 2016). More cortical drive is required to recruit the same motor pool. There is also evidence of altered intracortical processing — changes in short-interval intracortical inhibition (SICI), an index of GABAA-mediated inhibitory interneuron activity within primary motor cortex.

Evidence status Reduced corticomotor excitability in CAI is a consistent finding across multiple laboratories and both target muscles. The SICI findings are less consistent — sample sizes are small, methodology varies, and directionality is not uniform across studies. State the corticomotor excitability reduction as established; state the intracortical inhibition changes as an active and unresolved area.

Sensory reweighting and the visual-dominance trap

Postural control integrates three afferent streams: somatosensory, vestibular and visual. When somatosensory input from the ankle is degraded by deafferentation, the CNS reweights toward the streams that still work. Neuroimaging and EEG work in CAI shows the pattern this produces: reduced reliance on somatosensory processing regions and increased activity in visual-motor and frontal planning regions for tasks that should be automated. Meta-analysed posturography confirms the behavioural consequence — individuals with CAI show a disproportionately large increase in postural sway when vision is removed compared with healthy controls (Song et al., 2016).

Restated plainly: the patient has offloaded ankle control from an automatic subcortical loop onto conscious visual monitoring and cortical motor planning.

The visual-dominance trap — this is why athletes re-injure In your clinic the patient stands on one leg under good lighting, with a stable gaze, with no cognitive load, and passes every test you give them.

On the court, vision is committed elsewhere — to the ball, the opponent, the space they are cutting into — and often at exactly the moment of highest mechanical demand: the contested rebound, the landing under a defender, the change of direction while tracking a pass. The visual stream that was substituting for degraded somatosensory input is withdrawn precisely when it is most needed. Control collapses, foot position at contact is wrong, and the ankle inverts.

Direct implications for how you program balance work:
• Balance training performed with the eyes fixed on the foot or on a static point reinforces visual dependence. It trains the compensation, not the deficit.
• Progression must force sensory reweighting back toward somatosensory input: eyes closed, stroboscopic or occluded vision, gaze decoupled from the task (head turns, tracking a moving target, catching while balancing), and dual-task cognitive load.
• The endpoint is not "can balance for 30 seconds." It is "can maintain foot and limb control while the visual and attentional systems are fully occupied by something else." That is the condition under which the injury occurs, so it is the condition under which the athlete must be tested.

Cortical smudging: when the brain loses the map of the foot

Corticospinal suppression describes a degraded output. Visual reweighting describes a substituted input. Both sit downstream of a third problem: the fidelity of the somatosensory representation itself.

The primary somatosensory cortex holds a somatotopic map — the sensory homunculus — in which adjacent body regions occupy adjacent cortical territory with defined borders. That map is not fixed hardware. It is maintained continuously by afferent traffic and by use, and it degrades when either is withdrawn. Degradation is not erasure. Representations enlarge, shift, and lose their borders, so that adjacent territories overlap and the cortex can no longer resolve which part of the periphery is reporting. The term borrowed from the spinal pain literature for this is cortical smudging (Wand et al., 2011); the original demonstration was in chronic low back pain, where the extent of S1 reorganisation scaled with chronicity (Flor et al., 1997), and it is best characterised in complex regional pain syndrome.

An ankle after ligamentous injury supplies exactly the two inputs that drive this. Mechanoreceptor loss within the ligament removes afferent traffic. Guarding, altered gait, reduced exposure to varied surfaces and a foot that stops being explored remove use. The measurable consequences at the ankle are consistent with map degradation:

  • Reduced plantar cutaneous sensitivity in chronic ankle instability — elevated light-touch detection thresholds compared with controls, associated with worse postural control (Powell et al., 2014).
  • Deficits that widen under cognitive load. Burcal and Wikstrom (2016) found plantar sensitivity differences between CAI, copers and controls that were amplified when attention was divided — the same pattern as the visual-dominance trap, from the input side. When cortical resources are committed elsewhere, a degraded map degrades further.
  • Afferent augmentation works acutely. Textured insoles, textured surfaces and ankle taping improve postural control measures in CAI within a single session. An intervention that adds nothing mechanical but improves control is evidence that the limiting factor was afferent, not structural.

Two-point discrimination as a bedside proxy

Two-point discrimination (TPD) is the minimum separation at which two simultaneous tactile stimuli are perceived as two rather than one. It is a crude instrument with a useful property: it indexes the resolution of the cortical representation, not merely peripheral receptor density. It requires no equipment beyond a caliper or a bent paperclip, and it takes under two minutes.

Absolute thresholds vary enormously by site — a few millimetres at the fingertip, roughly 15–25 mm on the plantar forefoot, and 30–40 mm or more over the lateral leg and hindfoot. Those numbers are not the measurement you care about. The measurement is the involved-to-uninvolved ratio. Each patient carries their own control limb.

StepMethod
SitesFour per limb, tested identically on both: anterolateral ankle over the ATFL, sinus tarsi, lateral heel, plantar first metatarsal head. Mark them so the retest is the same skin.

The heuristic — use it, and know what it is If the patient cannot resolve two points on the lateral ankle at a separation their other side resolves easily, the cortex does not have a usable spatial representation of that joint. Feedforward control cannot be built on a map that blurred — and no amount of strength will substitute, because strength is an output problem and this is an input problem.

Chapter 6 establishes that protection has to be committed before contact. A feedforward command specifies where the foot will be at contact. Specifying a position requires knowing the current one to a spatial precision the smudged map cannot deliver. This is the mechanistic account of the clinically familiar patient: full strength, full range, no laxity, excellent single-leg balance in clinic, still turning the ankle on kerbs.

What it changes: the rehabilitation adds a sensory-discrimination component rather than more load. Tactile localisation ("where did I touch?"), graphesthesia (numbers traced on the skin, identified without vision), TPD training toward the uninvolved-side threshold, textured surface exposure — and, critically, these performed during weight-bearing tasks rather than as a seated add-on, because the map you want restored is the one used under load.
Evidence status — read this before you use TPD as an outcome Three separate claims are being stacked here and they are not equally supported. Reduced plantar cutaneous sensitivity in CAI is demonstrated. Cortical reorganisation after musculoskeletal injury is demonstrated — but chiefly in chronic low back pain and CRPS, not in ankle instability, where direct S1 mapping studies are sparse. The causal chain from smudged map to failed feedforward control to re-injury is a hypothesis, coherent with the corticospinal and sensory-reweighting data but not demonstrated prospectively at the ankle.

TPD itself deserves scepticism as a measurement: reliability varies markedly by body site and by protocol, and it is poorer at the foot and back than at the hand (Catley et al., 2013). Standardise the method or the numbers are noise. There is no established minimal clinically important difference for ankle TPD, so treat it as a within-patient asymmetry trend, not a discharge criterion.

And take care with the explanation you give. "Your brain has lost the map of your foot" is accurate enough as mechanism and frightening as a sentence. The usable version is that the foot has stopped sending clear information, that this is trainable, and that the training is the part of rehabilitation that most people skip.

Electromechanical delay and why reaction time is the wrong target

Chapter 6 establishes that fibularis reflex latency (~54–80 ms) cannot beat a 40–50 ms injury event. The real gap is larger than that comparison suggests, because the published latency figures measure the wrong endpoint.

Almost all "peroneal reaction time" data report the onset of the EMG signal. EMG onset is not force. Between electrical activation and mechanical torque at the joint lies the electromechanical delay (EMD) — excitation–contraction coupling, cross-bridge formation, and, dominantly, the time required to take up series elastic slack in tendon and aponeurosis. EMD at the ankle is on the order of 30–50 ms, and it lengthens with fatigue, with a more compliant tendon, and with low pre-activation.

No amount of reaction-time training closes a gap of this size, and the fact that this was the dominant rehabilitation model for two decades is a useful lesson in what happens when a plausible mechanism is never checked against the timescale of the actual event.

Compartment physics: chronic exertional compartment syndrome

Muscle volume increases by up to roughly 20% during exercise as blood flow and interstitial fluid rise. Each compartment of the leg is bounded by non-compliant fascia. When fascial compliance is insufficient for the volume increase, intramuscular pressure rises. As interstitial pressure approaches local capillary perfusion pressure, transmural pressure gradient collapses, capillary flow falls, and the tissue becomes relatively ischaemic — producing the characteristic crescendo tightness, aching and neurological symptoms that appear at a reproducible point in the exercise bout and resolve within minutes of stopping.

Diagnosis rests on intramuscular pressure measurement against the modified Pedowitz criteria (Pedowitz et al., 1990). Any one of the three is considered diagnostic:

Measurement pointDiagnostic threshold
Resting (pre-exercise) pressure≥ 15 mmHg
1 minute post-exercise≥ 30 mmHg
5 minutes post-exercise≥ 20 mmHg

The anterior compartment is most commonly involved, and because the deep fibular nerve runs within it, presentation often includes dorsal foot paraesthesia and late-run foot slap alongside anterior shin tightness. Deep posterior compartment involvement produces medial leg symptoms and can be difficult to separate from medial tibial stress syndrome.

Differential — the pattern separates them without imaging CECS: reproducible at a predictable distance or time into exercise, escalates while running, forces cessation, resolves within minutes to tens of minutes of stopping, non-tender at rest, often bilateral, frequently with neurological symptoms.
Medial tibial stress syndrome: diffuse tenderness along the posteromedial tibial border ≥5 cm, often warms up during exercise, tender at rest.
Tibial bone stress injury: focal tenderness, pain progressively earlier in the session across weeks, may persist at rest and at night, can hurt on hopping.

Asking "how long after you stop does it take to settle?" separates CECS from the bone problems faster than any test you can perform.
Threshold caveat The Pedowitz thresholds are the accepted reference standard but have imperfect diagnostic accuracy. Asymptomatic athletes can exceed them, and symptomatic patients can fall below them. Treat the numbers as confirmatory in the context of a characteristic exertional history, not as a stand-alone diagnostic test.

Baxter's nerve: the heel pain that is not plantar fasciitis

The first branch of the lateral plantar nerve — the inferior calcaneal nerve, or Baxter's nerve — arises from the lateral plantar nerve near the bifurcation of the tibial nerve. Its course contains two constriction points, and the anatomy is what makes the diagnosis:

  1. The nerve turns from a vertical to a horizontal course and passes between the deep fascia of abductor hallucis and the medial caudal margin of quadratus plantae. This is the principal entrapment site.
  2. It then runs laterally across the plantar foot, passing anterior to the medial calcaneal tuberosity, beneath the long plantar ligament — a second site of compression, particularly where there is a plantar calcaneal spur or a thickened, oedematous plantar fascia.

It supplies motor innervation to abductor digiti minimi (and contributions to flexor digitorum brevis and quadratus plantae) and sensory innervation to the calcaneal periosteum and long plantar ligament. It has no cutaneous sensory territory.

The trap that makes this the most-missed heel diagnosis Because Baxter's nerve carries no cutaneous sensation, entrapment produces no numbness on the skin. Clinicians exclude a nerve lesion on the basis of intact plantar sensation and default to plantar fasciitis. That reasoning is invalid here. Estimates attribute up to roughly 20% of chronic recalcitrant plantar heel pain to this nerve — and it frequently coexists with plantar fasciitis rather than replacing it, since fascial thickening and heel oedema compress the nerve at its second site.

Vascular anatomy

  • Posterior tibial artery — behind the medial malleolus, divides into medial and lateral plantar arteries. Palpable pulse; the main plantar supply.
  • Dorsalis pedis (continuation of anterior tibial) — lateral to EHL tendon on the dorsum. Absent congenitally in a minority of people, so a missing pulse is not automatically pathological — compare sides and correlate.
  • Fibular artery — supplies the lateral compartment and contributes to the talar and Achilles supply.

The Achilles has a relatively hypovascular zone roughly 2–6 cm proximal to its insertion, historically invoked to explain mid-portion tendinopathy. Modern imaging shows this region is not truly avascular and that tendinopathic tendons are typically hypervascular (neovascularisation). Treat the "watershed" story as historical, not mechanistic.

Self-test — Chapter 5

  1. An athlete six weeks after a lateral sprain has no laxity, full range, and burning on the dorsum of the foot. What is your leading hypothesis?
    Traction neuropathy of the superficial fibular nerve at its fascial exit ~10–12 cm above the lateral malleolus. Confirm with sensory mapping over the dorsum (sparing the 1st web space, which is deep fibular), Tinel's at the exit point, and a nerve tension test combining plantarflexion and inversion.
  2. Why is single-leg balance impaired on the uninjured side after a unilateral ankle sprain?
    Because the sensorimotor consequences of the injury are not purely local. Deafferentation and altered afferent processing produce central reorganisation — reduced corticospinal excitability and altered postural control strategies — which appear bilaterally. It is direct evidence that ankle instability is a neurological problem as much as a ligamentous one.
  3. Define arthrogenic muscle inhibition and give one clinical implication.
    Reflex inhibition of muscles crossing an injured joint, driven by altered afferent input and effusion, producing reduced voluntary activation independent of pain. Implication: strength deficits may not respond to load alone. You must address activation — effusion management, afferent input (manual, textured, external focus cues), and high-quality low-threshold recruitment — before or alongside progressive loading.

• Where does the CoP exit? Normal exit is under the hallux/1st metatarsal head. Any other exit point is a failure of the medial column to accept propulsive load.
• How fast does it cross the midfoot? A slow, dwelling midstance phase is normal. A midfoot phase that is fast and medially displaced indicates the foot never became a stable platform.
• When does the medial shift happen? Late or absent medial shift means the supination conversion did not occur, regardless of what the rearfoot looks like on video.

The lateral CoP shift of functional hallux limitus

Chapter 1 established the first-ray coupling and Chapter 6's windlass section explains why hallux dorsiflexion is the trigger. The CoP trace is where that failure becomes measurable. In functional hallux limitus — where the hallux has adequate non-weight-bearing range but cannot dorsiflex under load, usually because a dorsiflexed and inverted first ray has raised the 1st MTP axis — the sequence is:

  1. Hallux dorsiflexion is mechanically obstructed at the moment load reaches the forefoot.
  2. The windlass fails to engage; the medial column stays compliant and cannot accept propulsive load.
  3. The CoP, unable to translate medially, is deflected laterally — dwelling under the 2nd–4th metatarsal heads and frequently exiting under the lateral rays rather than the hallux.
  4. Because the medial lever is unavailable, the body shortens the lever arm instead: the heel leaves the ground earlier to generate a plantarflexion moment through the forefoot — premature heel-off.
The signature, and what it costs Signature on a pressure plate: laterally deviated CoP through terminal stance, reduced or absent hallux peak pressure, elevated 2nd and 3rd metatarsal head pressures, and a shortened forefoot contact time on the medial side.

Signature on visual gait analysis (no plate required): early heel rise, an apparent "toe-off over the outside of the foot," and a visibly abducted foot progression angle used to swing the body over the lateral column instead of rolling over the hallux.

What it costs, in order of appearance: lesser metatarsal overload and 2nd metatarsal bone stress injury → sustained plantar fascia strain from a windlass that never completes → increased plantarflexor demand and Achilles load from premature heel-off → and, over time, progressive dorsal 1st MTP osteophyte formation converting functional hallux limitus into structural hallux rigidus. Testing hallux dorsiflexion weight-bearing, with load through the first ray, is a ten-second examination that prevents this entire sequence being missed.

The windlass mechanism

Described by Hicks (1954). At terminal stance the MTP joints dorsiflex. Because the plantar aponeurosis inserts distal to the MTP axes, it winds around the metatarsal heads, shortening the distance between calcaneus and forefoot. Result: the arch rises, the calcaneus inverts, the midfoot supinates, and the foot converts into a rigid lever. Hallux dorsiflexion is the trigger.

Reason to test the big toe on every foot Loss of hallux dorsiflexion (hallux rigidus, turf toe, post-immobilisation stiffness, first-ray instability) disables the windlass. The foot never becomes a rigid lever, push-off occurs through a compliant midfoot, and load redistributes — producing lesser metatarsal overload, plantar fascia strain, and a shortened, low-power push-off. Functional requirement at push-off is roughly 60–65° of hallux dorsiflexion. Test it weight-bearing, not just on the plinth: an apparently mobile hallux non-weight-bearing can be functionally rigid under load, which is the "functional hallux limitus" pattern.

The foot as a spring

Ker et al. (1987) demonstrated that the human arch stores and returns elastic strain energy: roughly 17% of the mechanical energy per running step is recovered from arch deformation, with the Achilles contributing roughly 35%. Together, more than half of the energy of running is passively recycled.

Implication: an excessively stiff foot has poor shock absorption; an excessively compliant one wastes energy and overloads soft-tissue restraints. There is no universally optimal arch, only an appropriate stiffness for the task and the tissue's tolerance. This is why prospective studies have repeatedly failed to establish static foot posture as a strong general injury predictor, despite the strength of clinical belief.

These signals are excellent for flagging a change worth examining and for quantifying exposure. They are not a substitute for putting hands on the ankle, and a normal dashboard has never excluded a diagnosis.

Mechanism of lateral ankle sprain — corrected

Update your model The textbook mechanism is "plantarflexion + inversion." Video analysis of real injuries (Fong et al., 2009; and subsequent laboratory-induced sprain work) shows the actual mechanism is dominated by excessive inversion combined with internal rotation of the foot, often at or near neutral dorsiflexion, with the ankle reaching peak inversion within roughly 40–50 ms of contact. Two consequences: (1) prevention and rehabilitation must address transverse-plane control, not just frontal-plane; (2) any protective mechanism that relies on reflex muscle response is too slow — fibularis latency is on the order of 54–80 ms. Protection has to be feedforward (pre-activation, limb positioning at contact), which is why the old "train peroneal reaction time" model has largely been abandoned.

Self-test — Chapter 6

  1. A recreational runner with mid-portion Achilles tendinopathy can perform 20 single-leg calf raises pain-free and asks to return to running. What is the flaw in using that as a criterion?
    A single-leg calf raise loads the Achilles at roughly 1–2× BW; running loads it at 6–8× BW at high rate, hundreds of times per kilometre. Endurance at low load says nothing about tolerance to high-magnitude, high-rate load. Criteria must include heavy-load capacity and progressive plyometric/energy-storage tolerance, plus a graded running exposure — not a bodyweight endurance test alone.
  2. Explain, mechanically, why a stiff big toe can cause plantar heel pain.
    Hallux dorsiflexion (~60–65° required) triggers the windlass. If it is restricted, the plantar fascia is loaded without the arch-raising, foot-stiffening effect completing; push-off happens through a compliant midfoot, so the fascia experiences sustained high tensile strain over a longer portion of stance and across more steps. Load accumulates at the calcaneal enthesis.
  3. Why is training peroneal reaction time an inadequate strategy for preventing ankle sprain recurrence?
    Peak inversion occurs within ~40–50 ms of the injurious contact, while fibularis reflex latency is ~54–80 ms. The reflex cannot arrive in time. Effective protection is feedforward — pre-activation, limb and foot positioning at contact, trunk and hip control determining where the foot lands — which is what balance and perturbation training actually improve.

Tendon and mechanotransduction

Mechanotransduction is the process by which mechanical load is converted into cellular response (Khan & Scott, 2009): mechanocoupling → cell–cell communication → effector response (protein synthesis). Load is the signal, not merely the stress.

Key kinetics after a loading bout (Magnusson et al., 2010):

  • Both collagen synthesis and degradation increase after exercise.
  • Degradation dominates for roughly the first 24–36 hours — net collagen balance is negative.
  • Net synthesis becomes positive at roughly 36–72 hours.

This is the physiological basis for spacing heavy tendon loading approximately every other day rather than daily, and for expecting a transient post-load worsening that is not tissue damage.

Where the evidence is weaker than commonly stated The specific superiority of eccentric loading for tendinopathy is not established. Alfredson's eccentric protocol was influential but comparative trials show heavy slow resistance and other progressive loading modes produce equivalent outcomes with better adherence. The active ingredient appears to be progressive, sufficiently heavy, adequately dosed load over months, not the contraction mode. Similarly, the "pain during exercise up to 5/10 is acceptable" rule is a pragmatic, widely used heuristic with limited direct evidence of superiority over pain-free loading — use it, but do not present it as established fact.

What tendinopathy is at the cellular level: the pericellular matrix shift

"Degeneration" is a description, not a mechanism. The mechanism is a tenocyte phenotype change that alters what the cell secretes into its own pericellular matrix.

Healthy tendon. Type I collagen fibrils are packed in a matrix whose dominant proteoglycan is decorin — a small leucine-rich proteoglycan that binds along the fibril surface and regulates fibril diameter, spacing and interfibrillar load transfer. Small, low water-binding, organising.

Tendinopathic tendon. Tenocytes become rounded and chondrocyte-like and upregulate the large aggregating proteoglycans — aggrecan and versican (Corps et al., 2006). These molecules carry dense, strongly negatively charged glycosaminoglycan side chains and are intensely hydrophilic. The cascade that follows is mechanical:

  1. Large hydrophilic proteoglycans accumulate in the matrix and bind water.
  2. Ground substance volume increases → the tendon swells. This is what you see on imaging: increased anterior-posterior diameter, hypoechoic region, increased signal on MRI.
  3. Fluid accumulation separates the collagen fibrils and disrupts their interfibrillar connections.
  4. Fibril dissociation degrades load sharing between fibrils. Load that was distributed across a coupled fibril population is now carried by fewer, less-connected elements.
  5. Strain concentrates locally, driving further cell response — a self-sustaining loop. Neovascularisation and accompanying nerve ingrowth follow.
Two things this explains that nothing else does Why the tendon is bigger but weaker. Cross-sectional area increases while mechanical competence per unit area falls, because the added volume is water and ground substance, not aligned load-bearing collagen. Never reassure a patient on the basis of tendon thickness.

Why anti-inflammatory strategies fail. The dominant process is a matrix and cell-phenotype problem, not a leukocyte-mediated inflammatory one. Corticosteroid injection reduces pain short-term and produces worse outcomes at 6–12 months in mid-portion Achilles and lateral elbow tendinopathy — consistent with suppressing a symptom while further impairing matrix synthesis.
Unresolved Whether the proteoglycan shift is a cause of mechanical failure or a consequence of it is not settled — the loop is self-reinforcing and the entry point is unknown. Separately, imaging severity correlates poorly with symptoms and with function: markedly abnormal tendons are common in asymptomatic athletes, and symptomatic tendons can look near-normal. Manage the patient's capacity and load tolerance, not the image.

The host is not neutral: pharmacological matrix disruptors

Everything above assumes a biologically competent host. Two drug classes actively degrade the matrix you are trying to load, and both do it through the same final pathway: upregulation of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), the zinc-dependent enzymes that cleave collagen and proteoglycan. In healthy tendon, MMP activity is balanced by tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs). These agents break the balance.

Fluoroquinolones

Ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, moxifloxacin and related agents are chelators of divalent cations, and the proposed mechanism runs through that chemistry: disruption of magnesium-dependent integrin signalling between tenocytes and their matrix, producing oxidative stress, tenocyte apoptosis, upregulated MMP-2, MMP-3 and MMP-13, and suppressed type I collagen and proteoglycan synthesis. The matrix is degraded faster than it is replaced.

Management differs from ordinary tendinopathy in one critical respect: during active fluoroquinolone-associated tendinopathy the matrix is enzymatically compromised, so the usual instruction to load into acceptable discomfort is not safe. Heavy loading is deferred, the drug is stopped in consultation with the prescriber, and progression is slower and more conservative than the tendon's symptom level would otherwise justify.

Corticosteroids

Glucocorticoids suppress tenocyte proliferation and collagen synthesis, reduce cell viability, disorganise collagen fibril architecture, and — as with fluoroquinolones — shift the MMP/TIMP balance toward degradation. In vitro and animal work consistently shows reduced tendon mechanical properties after exposure; the effect is dose-dependent and worsened by repeated injection.

The clinical pattern follows precisely from that biology: good short-term pain relief, worse medium-term outcomes. In mid-portion Achilles tendinopathy and lateral elbow tendinopathy, injected cohorts do better at 4–6 weeks and worse at 6–12 months than exercise-treated cohorts. Chapter 7's earlier point is now mechanistic rather than empirical: an anti-inflammatory intervention is being applied to a matrix-synthesis problem, and it suppresses synthesis.

Do not overgeneralise this The evidence against corticosteroid is strongest for tendinopathy. It does not transfer wholesale to every peritendinous or intra-articular indication — injection into a stenosing tenosynovitis, a compressive nerve entrapment sheath, or an inflamed joint is a different target with a different evidence base. The rule to carry is specific: steroid into a degenerative, load-bearing tendon substance trades short-term comfort for medium-term mechanical competence.

Bone

Bone responds to strain magnitude, rate and distribution, and it desensitises quickly — most of the osteogenic signal from a loading bout is achieved within a small number of cycles, with diminishing returns thereafter, and recovery of sensitivity requires rest. Bone stress injury exists on a continuum from stress reaction to complete fracture. Two categories matter clinically and you must separate them from the first consultation:

Frost's mechanostat: the strain thresholds

Bone adaptation is governed by strain magnitude, expressed in microstrain (με; 1,000 με = 0.1% deformation). Frost's mechanostat defines discrete windows, each with a different biological response (Frost, 2003):

Disuse< ~50–100 μεRemodelling removes bone; net loss (bed rest, immobilisation, offloading)

Two clinical facts follow. First, the adaptive window is narrow — roughly a two-fold band between "does nothing" and "causes damage." Second, offloading is not neutral. Placing a patient in a boot drops strain below the disuse threshold and initiates bone loss; the tissue you are protecting is simultaneously weakening. Immobilisation duration is always a trade-off, never a safe default.

The BMU porosity window — why bone stress injury appears weeks after the error

Microdamage triggers targeted remodelling by Basic Multicellular Units — coupled teams of osteoclasts and osteoblasts that excavate and replace damaged bone. The sequence is fixed, and its order is the whole clinical point:

Resorption precedes formation. There is therefore an unavoidable window of several weeks during which the bone's response to a training stimulus has made it more fragile, not less. If loading continues at or above the provoking level through that window, adjacent resorption cavities coalesce and the microdamage becomes a macroscopic defect: stress reaction, then stress fracture.

This is the single most useful concept in load management It explains the pattern every clinician sees and few can account for: the athlete gets injured 2–4 weeks after the training error, not during it. The spike week feels fine. The bone is not responding yet. Symptoms emerge during the porosity window, by which time the athlete has "proved" to themselves that the load was tolerable and has usually continued or increased it.

Three consequences for practice:
• When you take a history for bone stress injury, interrogate the training log 2–6 weeks before symptom onset, not the week of onset. The causal event is upstream.
• "It felt fine last week" is not evidence of tolerance. It is the expected feature of the mechanism.
• Two to four weeks of relative rest is not clinical conservatism — it is a period matched to the resorption phase. Loading through it is loading a cortex that is measurably more porous than it was before training.

The same logic sets the ceiling on progression rate. Load must advance slowly enough that each increment's remodelling response completes before the next arrives.

RED-S: when the formation phase never arrives

The BMU model above assumes the osteoblastic formation phase is biologically funded. Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport is the condition in which it is not.

The pathway is endocrine. Sustained low energy availability — energy intake minus exercise energy expenditure, relative to fat-free mass — triggers a coordinated downregulation of non-essential physiological systems. The bone-relevant consequences:

  • Suppression of hypothalamic GnRH pulsatility → reduced LH/FSH → hypoestrogenism (functional hypothalamic amenorrhoea) or reduced testosterone in males. Oestrogen restrains osteoclastic activity; without it, resorption is disinhibited.
  • Reduced IGF-1 and altered leptin, triiodothyronine and cortisol profiles → impaired osteoblastic bone formation.
  • Frequently, inadequate calcium and vitamin D → impaired mineralisation of whatever osteoid is laid down.
Map this onto the BMU cycle and the danger becomes obvious Resorption proceeds. Formation does not follow. The 2–4 week porosity window — normally transient — is not closed. Resorption cavities persist and accumulate rather than being refilled, so each successive training stimulus excavates further into a cortex that never recovered from the last one.

This is why bone stress injuries in the energy-deficient athlete are multiple, recurrent, at unusual sites, and unresponsive to load management alone. You can dose the training load perfectly and the athlete will keep breaking, because the limiting factor is not mechanical.

The clinical rule: any athlete with a bone stress injury that is recurrent, bilateral, at a high-risk or atypical site, or occurring at loads their training history should tolerate, requires screening for low energy availability — menstrual history in females, libido and morning erections in males, weight and body composition trajectory, dietary restriction patterns, and referral for medical and dietetic assessment. Rehabilitation that does not address energy availability in these athletes will fail, and the failure will be attributed to the loading program.
Handle this history carefully Screening for low energy availability means asking about eating and body weight, which is clinically necessary and personally sensitive. Ask factually, without commentary on the athlete's body, and refer to a physician and a registered dietitian rather than attempting to manage intake yourself. Volume 7 covers the screening tools and the referral thresholds.

The immature skeleton: apophyseal mechanics

The mechanostat does not describe a child's foot. Before physeal closure, the weakest structure in the kinetic chain is not bone and not tendon — it is the cartilaginous apophysis, the traction growth plate where tendon inserts. Cartilage and osteochondral lesions

Articular cartilage is avascular, aneural and alymphatic, with very limited intrinsic repair. Lesions that breach subchondral bone recruit marrow elements and heal with fibrocartilage — mechanically inferior to hyaline cartilage. Osteochondral lesions of the talus are present in a meaningful proportion of ankles after significant sprain or fracture, and are a leading explanation for the persistently painful ankle with deep, load-related joint-line pain, catching, or recurrent effusion.

Self-test — Chapter 7

  1. Why is heavy tendon loading typically prescribed every other day?
    Collagen degradation exceeds synthesis for approximately the first 24–36 hours after loading; net synthesis becomes positive around 36–72 hours. Daily heavy loading risks stacking bouts within the net-catabolic window. Alternate-day heavy loading allows the anabolic phase to be expressed.
  2. A patient is 8 weeks post grade II lateral sprain, pain-free, full range. Are they healed?
    The ligament is in remodelling, not finished — type III to type I conversion and cross-linking continue for 12 months or more, and healed ligament reaches only ~50–70% of original tensile strength. Symptom resolution is not tissue capacity. Progression must be criterion-based (strength, hop testing, dynamic balance, sport-specific control), and maintenance training should continue well beyond symptom resolution.
  3. A runner has anterior tibial shaft pain and a footballer has medial forefoot pain over the 3rd metatarsal shaft. Why do these get managed differently?
    The anterior tibial cortex is a high-risk, tension-side bone stress site with real risk of progression to complete fracture and non-union — it needs imaging, offloading and specialist referral. The 3rd metatarsal shaft is a low-risk compression-side site that generally responds to load modification and graded return.

Chapter 8Why ankles fail

This chapter is the bridge to the rest of the curriculum. Everything above describes a working system. Here is how it stops working.

The epidemiological picture

  • Lateral ankle sprain is the most common musculoskeletal injury in sport and physical activity, with the highest rates in indoor and court sports (basketball, volleyball, handball, indoor football).
  • Roughly half of people who sprain an ankle never seek professional care, which is why the injury is simultaneously the commonest and the most undertreated.
  • Up to 40% of those who sustain a lateral sprain go on to develop persistent symptoms or chronic ankle instability.
  • Around 70–78% of ankle osteoarthritis is post-traumatic — the opposite pattern to hip and knee OA, where primary/idiopathic disease dominates.
The clinical thesis of this curriculum Those four facts, taken together, define your professional opportunity. Ankle sprain is treated as trivial by patients and often by clinicians. It is not: it is a high-recurrence injury with a defined route to joint degeneration, and it is undertreated. The clinician who rehabilitates ankles properly is preventing arthritis, not just returning someone to sport.

Copers versus chronic ankle instability

After identical injuries, some people return to full function with no recurrence ("copers") and others develop chronic ankle instability. Copers are not simply less severely injured — they display measurably different movement strategies, including altered landing kinematics and more distributed control. Studying copers, rather than only comparing CAI to uninjured controls, is the more informative research design and the one that has shaped current rehabilitation.

Arthrokinematic restriction — the specific one

People with CAI commonly show restricted posterior glide of the talus and consequently restricted dorsiflexion. This is not a general stiffness: the talus sits relatively anteriorly, limiting the arthrokinematic roll-and-glide required for dorsiflexion. It responds better to a mobilisation with movement (anteroposterior talar glide applied during weight-bearing dorsiflexion) than to calf stretching, because the restriction is at the joint, not in the muscle. Evidence supports short-term dorsiflexion gains; the durability of those gains is less certain, so it is an adjunct that opens a window for loading, not a treatment in itself.

The failure cascade — the mental model to keep

  1. Injury — collagen disruption and simultaneous mechanoreceptor loss.
  2. Effusion and pain → arthrogenic muscle inhibition; fibularis and soleus underactivate.
  3. Degraded afferent input → impaired joint position sense, altered postural control, bilateral central changes.
  4. Restricted posterior talar glide → reduced dorsiflexion → altered landing and gait strategy, compensatory pronation and proximal rotation.
  5. Altered feedforward control → the foot arrives at contact in a more inverted position → recurrence.
  6. Repeated microtrauma → osteochondral lesion, synovitis, altered contact stress.
  7. Post-traumatic ankle osteoarthritis.

Each arrow in that cascade is an intervention point. Volume 2 attacks the whole chain.

The 90-second differential: the lateral sprain that has not healed at twelve weeks

This is the single most common referral in ankle practice, and the great majority of the time it is one of eight things. The algorithm below is a hypothesis-ordering tool: it is designed to be run inside the subjective plus three loaded provocations, and it is called the 90-second differential because that is roughly how long it takes to get to a shortlist of two. Volume 1 develops it properly, with likelihood ratios attached to each test.

One precondition governs everything that follows. Before you look for a missed diagnosis, establish that the ankle has actually been rehabilitated — the commonest reason a sprain has not resolved at twelve weeks is not an occult lesion, it is twelve weeks of range of motion and balance work with no loading progression.

Diagnostic algorithm · Chapter 8

The lateral sprain that won't heal at 12 weeks

Entry criterion: documented inversion mechanism ≥ 12 weeks ago, still symptomatic or still limited.

Gate 0 — Has this ankle been loaded, or only rehabilitated? If loading never progressed beyond bodyweight, balance and range: this is under-dosed rehabilitation, not a missed diagnosis. Load it properly for six weeks and re-enter this algorithm only if it fails.
Screen first: night pain unrelated to position, non-mechanical pain, constitutional symptoms, inability to weight-bear four steps, focal bony tenderness on the posterior edge of either malleolus, or a mechanism that does not match the presentation. Any of these leaves the algorithm and goes to imaging or referral.
Node 1 — Where is the pain, and what provokes it?Ask the patient to point with one finger, then load the ankle three ways: deep weight-bearing dorsiflexion, single-leg hop, and resisted eversion in plantarflexion.
Branch A — Anterolateral, "pinching," worse in dorsiflexion
Anterolateral soft-tissue impingement / Bassett's ligament Pinch at end-range dorsiflexion, deep squat and stairs. Tenderness in the anterolateral gutter. No effusion, no laxity, no mechanical locking. Confirms: pain reproduced by gutter palpation combined with dorsiflexion; impingement pain unchanged by a posterior talar glide. Refutes: soft, symmetrical end-feel; pain abolished by the glide. Next: MRI has limited sensitivity for gutter scar — a negative scan does not exclude it. Treat conservatively; arthroscopy is both confirmatory and definitive if it fails.
Anterior bony impingement (tibiotalar osteophyte) Hard, abrupt, early end-feel. Often painless at rest, blocked in the lunge, and long-standing. Confirms: lateral radiograph in maximal dorsiflexion showing tibial or talar neck spurring. Next: manage the loss of range and its downstream cost; the osteophyte is the record of the problem, not the target.
Restricted posterior talar glide — mechanical, not pathological Dorsiflexion loss with anterior pain, calf stretching has changed nothing over months. Confirms: immediate range gain and pain reduction with a weight-bearing mobilisation with movement. Next: treat and consolidate with loading in the same session. Do not image this.
Branch B — Deep, poorly localised ache, with swelling after activity
Osteochondral lesion of the talus Deep ache the patient cannot point to precisely, effusion the day after load, sometimes catching or a sense of the joint "sticking." Talar cartilage is roughly 60% of the bone's surface and heals poorly — this is the classic "sprain that never got better." Confirms: MRI. Plain films are frequently normal early and their normality has misled the previous three clinicians. Refutes: pain that is superficial, palpable and reproducible by direct ligament palpation. Next: imaging now, not another loading block. Note the second-order effect: recurrent effusion sustains arthrogenic inhibition, so the strength deficit will not resolve until the joint does.
Occult fracture — stop and image Persistent focal bony tenderness at any of: lateral process of the talus, anterior process of the calcaneus, base of the fifth metatarsal, posterior talar process or os trigonum, navicular. Confirms: CT where plain films are normal and focal bony tenderness persists at twelve weeks. Next: these are the fractures routinely missed on the initial radiographs of a "simple sprain." Focal bony tenderness at three months is a bone diagnosis until proven otherwise.
Branch C — Lateral hindfoot / sinus tarsi, worse on uneven ground
Sinus tarsi syndrome Tenderness in the sinus itself, a sense of instability without true giving way, symptoms strongly surface-dependent, often relieved by subtalar-directed taping. Confirms: local tenderness plus symptom relief on a subtalar-controlling tape or a diagnostic injection. Next: subtalar control and afferent loading on varied surfaces, not more talocrural work.
Fibularis tendon pathology — brevis split tear or subluxation Retromalleolar or distal tendon pain, sometimes a snap or a click, swelling that follows the tendon rather than the joint. Confirms: tenderness along the tendon course, pain and weakness on resisted eversion in plantarflexion, dynamic ultrasound for subluxation — a static image will miss it. Next: assess the superior peroneal retinaculum. Recurrent subluxation is a surgical conversation.
Chronic ankle instability itself — no new structure Everything above negative. Deficits sit in the sensory-perceptual and motor-behavioural domains: low CAIT, asymmetric hop battery, dorsiflexion restriction, poor eyes-closed and dual-task control. Confirms: the pattern is a capacity and control deficit, not a pain generator. Next: this is the largest group. Treat the whole failure cascade above, not the residual soreness.
Branch D — Burning, tingling, hypersensitivity
Superficial fibular nerve traction neuropathy Burning or altered sensation over the dorsum, sparing the first web space; pain out of proportion to the mechanical findings; imaging normal. Confirms: Tinel's at the fascial exit 10–12 cm above the lateral malleolus, sensory mapping, and a tension test in plantarflexion with inversion. Refutes: a sensory deficit including the first web space — that is deep fibular and a different problem. Next: nerve-directed management. Continued ligament loading will not touch this and may aggravate it.
Branch E — Pain on rotation and push-off, higher than expected
Missed or under-treated syndesmotic injury Tenderness proximal to the ATFL, pain provoked by external rotation and by dorsiflexion, painful single-leg hop, recovery timeline several times longer than expected for a lateral sprain. Confirms: external rotation stress test, squeeze test, length of proximal tenderness; weight-bearing CT or stress radiographs where instability is suspected. Next: examine the medial side before you conclude. Medial tenderness alongside a syndesmotic injury raises deltoid disruption, and 1 mm of lateral talar shift removes roughly 40% of tibiotalar contact area — that finding changes management entirely.
Branch F — Plantar or plantar-lateral, appearing weeks after the sprain
Downstream load-redistribution pain The sprain settled and something else started. Plantar fascia strain, lesser metatarsal overload, or calcaneal stress reaction, arising because the foot has been offloading the lateral column and pushing off through an altered centre of pressure for three months. Confirms: weight-bearing hallux dorsiflexion, windlass test, and a pressure or CoP assessment if available; palpate for the pattern rather than the point. Next: treat the ankle that caused it. If the plantar heel pain is medial and resistant, screen Baxter's nerve before escalating.
Terminal node — everything above negative. Then the diagnosis is that the ankle was never restored to the demand it is being asked to meet. Test capacity rather than tissue: dorsiflexion symmetry on the weight-bearing lunge, heel-raise repetitions to fatigue, a hop battery for limb symmetry, and landing quality under gaze-decoupled and dual-task conditions. The commonest cause of a twelve-week failure is not an exotic lesion. It is an ankle discharged on the basis of what it can do while the patient is paying attention to it.
First 30 s — historyOnset, mechanism, what makes it better, and one verbatim descriptor. Surface-dependent, attention-dependent, or time-of-day-dependent all discriminate.
Next 30 s — palpation mapAnterolateral gutter, sinus tarsi, fibularis tendons, AITFL, the five occult fracture sites, medial gutter.
Last 30 s — three loaded testsWeight-bearing lunge to end range, single-leg hop, resisted eversion in plantarflexion — with external rotation stress if Branch E is live.
What this algorithm is and is not It is a structured ordering of hypotheses built from the mechanisms in this volume. It is not a validated clinical prediction rule, no branch carries a published likelihood ratio here, and the branches are not mutually exclusive — impingement coexisting with an osteochondral lesion, or Baxter's coexisting with plantar fasciitis, is common rather than exceptional. Use it to make sure you have considered the eight, not to conclude one. Volume 1 rebuilds it with test statistics attached and Volume 2 rebuilds the treatment.

Self-test — Chapter 8

  1. A patient has a grossly positive anterior drawer, plays basketball, and reports no episodes of giving way in three years. What is your management?
    Laxity is not instability — correlation between mechanical laxity and functional instability is poor. If sensory-perceptual and motor-behavioural domains test well (normal balance, hop symmetry, high CAIT, no perceived instability, good landing quality), this is a coper with a lax ligament. Do not treat the imaging or the drawer test. Confirm capacity, address any measurable deficit, and monitor.
  2. Why does calf stretching often fail to restore dorsiflexion in chronic ankle instability?
    The restriction is frequently arthrokinematic — restricted posterior talar glide with a relatively anterior talus — not myofascial. Stretching targets muscle length and does not address the joint glide, so range does not change. A weight-bearing mobilisation with movement applying an anteroposterior talar glide during active dorsiflexion targets the actual restriction; expect short-term gains that must be immediately consolidated with loading.
  3. Name the three domains of the CAI model and give one measurement for each.
    Pathomechanical — anterior drawer / weight-bearing lunge test for dorsiflexion. Sensory-perceptual — Y-balance or SEBT reach, CAIT score. Motor-behavioural — single-leg hop battery, calf-raise endurance, landing quality assessment.
  4. Why is the coper an important comparison group?
    Because comparing CAI to uninjured controls only tells you what injury changed. Comparing CAI to copers — people who sustained the same injury and recovered fully — isolates the factors that distinguish successful adaptation from failed adaptation, which are the modifiable targets for rehabilitation.

Case A

The calf that dies at four kilometres

A 34-year-old recreational marathon runner, 62 km/week for three years, presents with an eight-month history of mid-portion Achilles symptoms. His primary complaint is not pain. He reports that the calf becomes "dead, heavy and burnt out" at around 4 km, after which his stride shortens and his pace drifts by 20–25 s/km despite unchanged effort. There is mild morning stiffness lasting five minutes and mild discomfort at the tendon mid-portion, but he rates pain 2/10 and it does not stop him running.

Previous management: a diagnosis of Achilles tendinitis, two weeks of NSAIDs, a course of ultrasound therapy, and a calf-strengthening program of three sets of 15 single-leg heel raises daily for four months. He was compliant. Nothing changed.

Examination. Tendon is palpably thickened over 3 cm at the mid-portion, minimally tender. He performs 26 single-leg heel raises to fatigue on the affected side, 28 on the unaffected side. Isometric plantarflexion strength is symmetrical. Weight-bearing lunge test 11 cm bilaterally. Single-leg hop distance symmetrical. On repeated single-leg hopping, contact time on the affected side is visibly longer and hop height lower; he describes the leg as feeling "soft" and "like landing in sand."

Reasoning questions

  1. His endurance testing is essentially symmetrical and his strength is normal. Why is he still failing at 4 km?
    Because the failing element is the tendon's spring function, not the muscle's force capacity. During running stance the triceps surae fascicles operate quasi-isometrically while the tendon and aponeurosis absorb and return essentially all muscle–tendon unit length change — the catapult. Heel-raise endurance tests slow, muscle-driven concentric work at low load and does not interrogate the spring at all. He can pass every muscle test he is given while the mechanism he actually runs on is broken.
  2. Explain, at the fascicle level, why the calf fatigues early.
    The tendinopathic tendon is more compliant. A tendon that is too compliant can no longer hold the fascicles near constant length under load, so the fascicles are forced to lengthen and shorten to complete each stride. They leave the high-force, metabolically cheap isometric region of the force–velocity relationship, move onto a less favourable part of the curve, and consume substantially more metabolic energy per step. Early fatigue and reduced propulsive output follow directly. The "dead calf" is a stiffness problem presenting as an endurance problem.
  3. What do the longer contact time, lower hop height and "landing in sand" sensation confirm?
    Reduced tendon stiffness and therefore reduced reactive strength. A compliant series-elastic element takes longer to take up slack and returns less stored energy, lengthening ground contact time and reducing rebound. It is the clinical expression of the same mechanism, and it is measurable in the room without equipment.
  4. Why did four months of high-repetition heel raises fail, and what should the loading target have been?
    Three sets of 15 bodyweight heel raises is low-magnitude, high-repetition work. Tendon stiffness adapts to high tensile load with sustained time under tension, not to repetition volume — so the intervention never applied the stimulus the impairment required. The target is heavy slow resistance: heavy load, controlled tempo, alternate-day frequency to respect the 24–36 hour net-catabolic collagen window, progressed over months, followed by graded energy-storage and plyometric loading to restore reactive function.
  5. Why were the NSAIDs and ultrasound irrelevant, and what would a corticosteroid injection have done?
    The pathology is a tenocyte phenotype and matrix problem — upregulated aggrecan and versican, water binding, fibril separation, degraded load sharing — not a leukocyte-mediated inflammatory one. Anti-inflammatory strategies target a process that is not driving the disorder. Corticosteroid would suppress tenocyte proliferation and collagen synthesis and shift the MMP/TIMP balance toward degradation: short-term pain relief, worse mechanical competence at 6–12 months, in a patient whose problem is already mechanical competence.
  6. His tendon is visibly thicker on imaging than the unaffected side. What does that tell you about its strength?
    Nothing reassuring. The added cross-sectional area is bound water and ground substance, not aligned load-bearing collagen — competence per unit area has fallen while area has risen. Imaging severity correlates poorly with symptoms and function in either direction. Manage capacity, not the image.
The transferable lesson When a patient's dominant complaint is fatigue, heaviness or loss of power rather than pain, and muscle testing is normal, suspect the elastic element rather than the contractile one. The tests that expose it are reactive — hopping contact time, hop height, rebound quality — not endurance counts.
Case B

The normal X-ray that was not normal

A 22-year-old semi-professional footballer, five months after an injury sustained when an opponent fell across his planted, dorsiflexed foot, forcing it into external rotation. He was diagnosed with an ankle sprain. Radiographs at presentation — AP, lateral and mortise, non-weight-bearing — were reported as normal, with tibiofibular clear space and overlap within normal limits. He completed six weeks of standard lateral sprain rehabilitation and returned to training.

He now reports two problems. First, sharp anterolateral ankle pain at the front of the joint, reproduced when he squats deeply or drives forward into a sprint start, occasionally with a catching sensation. Second, and more troubling to him, the ankle feels unstable specifically when he turns — pivoting on the planted foot, cutting, or changing direction — but never when running straight and never on uneven ground.

Examination. Anterior drawer and talar tilt are negative. Weight-bearing lunge test 13 cm on the affected side, 12 cm unaffected — dorsiflexion is not restricted, but end-range dorsiflexion reproduces his sharp anterolateral pain. Point tenderness is localised to the anterolateral gutter. Squeeze test equivocal. External rotation stress test reproduces deep anterolateral discomfort. Single-leg rotational hop is markedly asymmetrical: he refuses to commit to the turn. MRI, obtained privately, was reported as showing no ligament tear and no bone marrow oedema.

Reasoning questions

  1. The mechanism was described as an "ankle sprain." What actually happened, and why does the mechanism alone rule out a lateral sprain?
    A planted, dorsiflexed foot forced into external rotation is a syndesmotic mechanism. The lateral ligament complex — ATFL in particular — is loaded in plantarflexion and inversion and is slack in dorsiflexion. The wide anterior trochlea driven into the mortise in dorsiflexion acts as a wedge forcing the tibia and fibula apart, which is precisely how the syndesmotic ligaments fail. The stated mechanism excludes the diagnosis he was given.
  2. His radiographs showed normal clear space and overlap. Why does that not exclude syndesmotic instability?
    Because syndesmotic instability in a shallow incisura is predominantly rotational and translational in the axial plane — the fibula externally rotates and translates posteriorly within the notch — rather than producing frank widening on an AP or mortise view. Coronal-plane measurements can be entirely normal while the fibula is malpositioned rotationally. The imaging required is axial CT with bilateral comparison, because incisura morphology is so individual that the uninjured side is the only valid reference.
  3. Subsequent CT shows a shallow, flat incisura fibularis bilaterally. How does that change your interpretation of the same ligament injury?
    Bony containment is minimal, so restraint against fibular external rotation and posterior translation is almost entirely ligamentous. The identical AITFL/IOL lesion that would be partially compensated in a deep, concave incisura produces substantially greater rotational instability here. It explains why his symptoms are strictly rotational — provoked by pivoting and cutting, absent in straight-line running — and why a standard six-week sprain protocol, which contains no rotational loading criteria, discharged him unprepared for the demand that actually destabilises him.
  4. Account for the sharp anterolateral pain at end-range dorsiflexion despite a normal MRI.
    Bassett's ligament — the distal accessory fascicle of the AITFL, present in most ankles as a normal variant. With syndesmotic and anterolateral instability the talus sits and translates anteriorly and rotates internally, presenting the anterolateral talar dome against a fascicle it did not previously contact. Dorsiflexion drags the fascicle across articular cartilage, producing abrasion and synovitis. MRI is reported as normal because the structure itself is normal tissue — the diagnosis is clinical and confirmed arthroscopically.
  5. Why do the negative anterior drawer and talar tilt tests carry so little weight here?
    They test the lateral ligament complex in the sagittal and frontal planes. His instability is transverse-plane rotational instability of the fibula within the incisura, which those tests do not load. Testing the wrong plane and finding nothing is not a negative finding — it is an untested hypothesis. The functionally informative test here is the asymmetrical single-leg rotational hop, and it is grossly positive.
  6. He asks whether a screw or a suture-button would be better if surgery is required. Answer mechanically.
    A rigid screw abolishes fibular migration — the external rotation, posterior translation and 1–2 mm proximal glide required for the mortise to widen in dorsiflexion — constraining the talocrural instantaneous axis and commonly producing a hard anterior block and impingement pain until removal or breakage. A dynamic suture-button resists diastasis while permitting that micromotion, generally restoring dorsiflexion earlier. However, reduction accuracy dominates construct choice: a malreduced suture-button is worse than a well-reduced screw. This is a surgical decision; your contribution is knowing what each construct will and will not permit during rehabilitation.
The transferable lesson Instability has a plane. Ask which movement provokes it — inversion on uneven ground, or rotation on a planted foot — and test that plane. A patient who is stable in straight-line running and unstable only when turning has not been examined until you have loaded him in rotation. And a normal coronal-plane radiograph is evidence about the coronal plane only.
Case C

It felt fine last week

A 19-year-old university middle-distance runner, female. Six weeks ago she moved from 45 km to 72 km/week within nine days, entering a training camp, and simultaneously added two hill sessions per week. She felt no symptoms during that period and reported the block as one of her best.

Symptoms began three weeks ago as a vague ache along the posteromedial tibia toward the end of runs. She saw a clinician who identified no focal tenderness, diagnosed medial tibial stress syndrome, advised her to "run through it if it warms up," and prescribed calf strengthening and a foam roller. She continued training at 70 km/week.

She now presents with pain that begins within the first kilometre, persists for several hours after running, and has twice woken her at night. There is focal tenderness over a 2 cm segment of the distal posteromedial tibia. Single-leg hopping reproduces sharp local pain at three repetitions.

Additional history, obtained on direct questioning. Her last menstrual period was fourteen months ago; she attributes this to training volume and has not sought advice. Her body mass has fallen 4 kg over eight months, which she describes as intentional "race weight" management. She sustained a second metatarsal stress fracture two years ago and a sacral stress reaction eighteen months ago, both attributed at the time to training error.

Reasoning questions

  1. She had no symptoms at all during the nine-day load spike. Explain why, in terms of the BMU cycle.
    Microdamage from the spike triggers targeted remodelling, and the BMU sequence is obligatory in order: activation over days, then osteoclastic resorption over roughly 2–4 weeks, then reversal, then osteoblastic formation over ~3 months, then mineralisation. Resorption precedes formation. During the spike itself the bone has not yet responded, so nothing hurts. Symptoms emerge weeks later during the resorption phase, when cortical porosity has transiently risen and the region is weaker than before the training stimulus.
  2. Where should the clinician have looked in her training history, and what was the specific reasoning error in "run through it if it warms up"?
    The causal event is upstream of symptom onset: interrogate the training log 2–6 weeks before symptoms began, not the week they appeared. The reasoning error is treating "it felt fine last week" and "it warms up" as evidence of tolerance. Asymptomatic tolerance during the spike is the expected feature of the mechanism, not reassurance. Continuing to load through the porosity window allows resorption cavities to coalesce, converting microdamage into a macroscopic defect: stress reaction, then stress fracture.
  3. Distinguish her presentation from medial tibial stress syndrome and from chronic exertional compartment syndrome using the history alone.
    Bone stress injury: focal tenderness over a short segment, pain arriving progressively earlier across weeks, persisting after exercise, night pain, positive hop test. MTSS: diffuse tenderness along ≥5 cm of the posteromedial border, frequently improves as the run continues, no night pain. CECS: reproducible at a predictable point in the session, escalates while running, is non-tender at rest, and settles within minutes to tens of minutes of stopping. Night pain and focal tenderness placed her outside MTSS at the first consultation.
  4. Fourteen months of amenorrhoea, intentional weight loss, and three bone stress injuries in two years. What is the unifying diagnosis and its mechanism?
    Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport. Sustained low energy availability suppresses hypothalamic GnRH pulsatility, reducing LH/FSH and producing hypoestrogenism — and oestrogen restrains osteoclastic activity, so resorption is disinhibited. Reduced IGF-1 and altered thyroid and cortisol profiles impair osteoblastic formation. Mapped onto the BMU cycle: resorption proceeds and formation does not follow. The porosity window never closes, cavities accumulate rather than refilling, and each training stimulus excavates further into a cortex that never recovered from the last.
  5. She asks for a return-to-running timeline based on symptom resolution. Why is that the wrong framework for her specifically?
    Symptom resolution reflects the settling of local irritation, not refilling of resorption cavities and mineralisation of new osteoid, which take months. In her case the formation phase is endocrinologically obstructed, so the tissue may never reach competence on the current biology regardless of how she feels or how carefully load is dosed. Progression must be criterion-based and, more importantly, contingent on restoring energy availability. A perfectly dosed loading program will still fail here, and the failure will be misattributed to the program.
  6. Her clinician proposes six weeks in a walking boot with complete offloading. Critique this using the mechanostat.
    Complete offloading drops local strain below the disuse threshold (<~50–100 με), where remodelling removes bone — so protection actively weakens the tissue, in an athlete whose formation phase is already impaired. Offloading is a trade-off, never a safe default. The aim is a period of relative rest sufficient to clear the resorption phase, followed by return to the modelling window (~1,500–3,000 με) via progressive, monitored loading — while the energy availability problem is addressed medically.
  7. What must happen before rehabilitation planning proceeds?
    Referral for medical assessment (endocrine and menstrual evaluation, bone mineral density, vitamin D and calcium status) and to a registered dietitian for energy availability assessment. Take the history factually — menstrual status, weight trajectory, dietary restriction — without commenting on her body, and do not attempt to manage intake yourself. Her bone problem is not primarily a mechanical problem, and treating it as one has already failed her twice.
The transferable lesson Bone stress injury is always two questions: was the mechanical load excessive, and was the biology capable of responding to it? A recurrent, bilateral, atypically sited, or low-load bone stress injury has answered the second question already. Screening for energy availability is not an optional extra in that population — it is the diagnosis.

AssessmentCumulative exam — Volume 0

Answer from memory before opening anything. Target: 16/20 to progress to Volume 1. Below 16, re-read the chapters that generated your errors before moving on.

  1. State the mean anterior–posterior width difference of the trochlea tali and derive three clinical consequences.
    ~2.4 mm wider anteriorly. Consequences: dorsiflexion is close-packed (bony stability, sprain unlikely); plantarflexion is loose-packed (ligament-dependent, sprain occurs here); the mortise must widen in dorsiflexion, so fibular migration is required and syndesmotic injury is a dorsiflexion/external-rotation event.
  2. Give the two angular components of the subtalar axis and explain what a more vertical axis does to load transmission.
    ~42° from the transverse plane, ~16–23° medial to the sagittal midline; wide individual variation (~20–68° inclination). A more vertical axis converts pronation into proportionally more transverse-plane motion — more tibial internal rotation transmitted proximally per degree of pronation.
  3. List the lateral ligament failure sequence with approximate failure loads.
    ATFL (~140 N) → CFL (~345 N) → PTFL (strongest). CFL is the only one crossing both talocrural and subtalar joints.
  4. State the relative contributions of PITFL, AITFL and IOL to syndesmotic stability.
    PITFL ~42%, AITFL ~35%, IOL ~22% (Ogilvie-Harris et al., 1994).
  5. Explain why 1 mm of lateral talar shift is clinically catastrophic.
    It reduces tibiotalar contact area by ~40%, concentrating contact stress over a smaller region and driving post-traumatic degeneration — which accounts for ~70–78% of ankle OA.
  6. Describe the windlass mechanism and state the hallux dorsiflexion required at push-off.
    MTP dorsiflexion winds the plantar aponeurosis around the metatarsal heads (it inserts distal to the MTP axes), shortening the calcaneus-to-forefoot distance, raising the arch, inverting the calcaneus, supinating the midfoot and creating a rigid lever. Requires ~60–65° hallux dorsiflexion.
  7. Why does the classic "plantarflexion + inversion" sprain mechanism need revising?
    Video analysis of real injuries shows the dominant components are excessive inversion plus internal rotation, often near neutral dorsiflexion, reaching peak inversion within ~40–50 ms. Transverse-plane control matters, and reflex-based protection is too slow.
  8. State fibularis reflex latency and the injury time-course, and derive the training implication.
    Latency ~54–80 ms; peak inversion within ~40–50 ms. The reflex arrives too late, so protection must be feedforward — pre-activation and limb positioning at contact — which is what balance and perturbation training develop.
  9. Give the collagen synthesis/degradation timeline after a heavy tendon loading bout.
    Degradation exceeds synthesis for ~24–36 h (net negative); net synthesis positive at ~36–72 h. Basis for alternate-day heavy loading.
  10. What percentage of original tensile strength does healed ligament reach at one year, and what does that mean for patient education?
    ~50–70% in animal models, with altered collagen organisation. Patients should be told the tissue does not fully return to baseline, so protection comes from capacity and control — justifying long-term maintenance training.
  11. List the three domains of the Hertel & Corbett CAI model with one measure each.
    Pathomechanical (laxity tests, weight-bearing lunge); sensory-perceptual (Y-balance, CAIT); motor-behavioural (hop battery, calf-raise endurance, landing quality).
  12. Distinguish mechanical from functional instability and state their correlation.
    Mechanical = measurable laxity beyond physiological limits; functional = subjective giving way and perceived instability. Correlation is poor; neither rules the other in or out.
  13. Name the nerve injured in ~10–25% of inversion sprains, its exit point and its sensory territory.
    Superficial fibular nerve; pierces deep fascia ~10–12 cm above the lateral malleolus; supplies the dorsum of the foot sparing the 1st web space (deep fibular).
  14. Define arthrogenic muscle inhibition and give the treatment implication.
    Reflex inhibition of muscles crossing an injured joint from altered afferent input and effusion, reducing voluntary activation independently of pain. Implication: manage effusion and restore activation quality alongside loading; load alone may not resolve the deficit.
  15. Give dorsiflexion requirements for walking, running and deep squat, plus the normative weight-bearing lunge distance.
    Walking ~10°, running ~20–25°, deep squat ~35–40°. Knee-to-wall ~9–12 cm; asymmetry >1.5 cm is the more meaningful finding.
  16. Rank the static contributors to medial arch stiffness with percentages.
    Plantar aponeurosis ~25%, long plantar ligament ~17%, spring ligament ~10% (Huang et al., 1993).
  17. Why is a non-weight-bearing radiograph inadequate to exclude a Lisfranc injury?
    There is no intermetatarsal ligament between the 1st and 2nd metatarsal bases; instability shows as 1st–2nd interspace widening, which only appears under load or stress. Non-weight-bearing films are commonly normal in subtle Lisfranc injuries.
  18. Explain why fibularis longus dysfunction can produce forefoot and plantar heel symptoms.
    It plantarflexes and everts the first ray, pressing the 1st metatarsal head into the ground. Weakness destabilises the first ray, compromises hallux dorsiflexion and windlass engagement, and redistributes load to the lesser rays and the plantar fascia.
  19. Separate high-risk from low-risk bone stress injury sites in the foot and ankle and say why the distinction matters.
    High-risk: navicular, 5th MT base (Jones zone), medial malleolus, talus, anterior tibial cortex, sesamoids. Low-risk: 2nd–4th metatarsal shafts, posteromedial tibia, calcaneus, fibula. High-risk sites are tension-sided or poorly vascularised, with real risk of non-union or progression to complete fracture; they need imaging, offloading and often surgical opinion rather than load modification alone.
  20. Give the failure cascade from acute lateral sprain to post-traumatic ankle osteoarthritis.
    Ligament and mechanoreceptor disruption → effusion and pain → arthrogenic muscle inhibition → degraded afferent input and impaired postural control (bilateral) → restricted posterior talar glide and reduced dorsiflexion → altered landing/gait strategy and altered feedforward control → recurrent sprain → osteochondral lesion, synovitis, altered contact stress → post-traumatic OA.
  21. Explain, from trochlear geometry alone, why dorsiflexion is obligatorily coupled with external rotation and abduction of the talus.
    The trochlea approximates a truncated cone with a medial apex, so the lateral margin has a larger radius of curvature and a longer arc than the medial. For a given sagittal rotation the lateral side must travel further; constrained within the mortise, the difference is expressed as yaw — external rotation and abduction. It is geometric, not muscular, and the instantaneous centre of rotation migrates through range accordingly (Barnett & Napier, 1952).
  22. Describe the facet curvatures of the subtalar joint and explain why supination stiffens the hindfoot.
    Posterior facet is convex on the calcaneus (concave on talus); anterior and middle facets are concave on the calcaneus (convex on talus). Oppositely curved surface pairs about one oblique axis make pure rotation impossible — the talus translates while rotating, a helical/screw path (Manter, 1941). In supination, facet congruency rises while the interosseous talocalcaneal and cervical ligaments wind and shorten, so stiffness climbs progressively. It is progressive stiffening, not a discrete lock.
  23. State Manter's two midtarsal axes and give the clinical distinction they permit.
    Longitudinal axis: ~15° inclination, ~9° deviation from sagittal — almost pure frontal-plane forefoot inversion/eversion. Oblique axis: ~52° inclination, ~57° deviation — coupled sagittal and transverse motion. Distinction: a forefoot varus/supinatus compensation occurs about the longitudinal axis and is treated with frontal-plane strategies; a "midfoot break" absorbing lost ankle dorsiflexion occurs about the oblique axis and is treated by restoring talocrural dorsiflexion.
  24. State the first ray axis orientation and the obligatory coupling, then explain why fibularis longus cannot be substituted.
    Axis inclined ~45°, running dorsal-medial-proximal to plantar-lateral-distal (Hicks, 1953). Coupling: dorsiflexion with inversion; plantarflexion with eversion. For the 1st metatarsal head to press flat into the ground at push-off the ray must plantarflex and evert. Fibularis longus is the only muscle producing both components, because it approaches from the plantar-lateral side through the cuboid groove, matching the axis. FHB and intrinsics plantarflex but cannot evert.
  25. Add electromechanical delay to the reaction-time argument. What total protective latency results, and what does that mandate?
    Published fibularis latency (~54–80 ms) measures EMG onset, not torque. EMD — excitation–contraction coupling plus take-up of series elastic slack — adds ~30–50 ms, giving ~85–130 ms to meaningful joint torque against a 40–50 ms injury. The reactive pathway cannot participate. Protection must be feedforward: foot and limb position at contact, pre-activation stiffness, and early (0–50 ms) rate of force development.
  26. Explain the visual-dominance trap and one concrete programming consequence.
    Degraded ankle somatosensory input causes sensory reweighting toward vision; CAI patients show disproportionate sway increase when vision is removed and greater visual-motor/frontal cortical activity for simple ankle tasks. In sport, vision is committed to the ball or opponent at the moment of highest mechanical demand, withdrawing the substituting stream exactly when needed. Consequence: balance training with gaze fixed on the foot reinforces the compensation; progression must use eyes closed, occluded/stroboscopic vision, gaze-decoupled tasks and cognitive dual-task load.
  27. Describe the catapult mechanism and explain how reduced tendon stiffness produces early calf fatigue.
    During running stance, triceps surae fascicles operate quasi-isometrically while the tendon and aponeurosis absorb and return essentially all MTU length change — muscle as anchor, tendon as spring. Isometric operation sits at the high-force, metabolically cheap region of the force–velocity relationship. If tendon stiffness falls (tendinopathy, immobilisation, post-repair elongation), the tendon can no longer hold the fascicles at operating length under load, so fascicles must lengthen and shorten. They move to a worse point on the force–velocity curve at higher metabolic cost, producing early fatigue — a stiffness problem presenting as an endurance problem.
  28. Explain mid-portion Achilles tendinopathy via inter-fascicular sliding, and state the evidence limitation.
    The Achilles comprises three subtendons that spiral ~90° and displace non-uniformly; sliding is permitted by the elastin- and lubricin-rich interfascicular matrix. Non-enzymatic glycation cross-links the IFM, reducing sliding and mechanically coupling the subtendons, so differential displacement converts into localised interfascicular shear strain — concentrated where the twist is greatest, 2–6 cm above the insertion. Limitation: demonstrated ex vivo and by imaging, but not shown causally in vivo in humans; it is the leading hypothesis, not established fact.
  29. Give the modified Pedowitz criteria and explain the hydrostatic mechanism.
    Resting ≥15 mmHg; 1 minute post-exercise ≥30 mmHg; 5 minutes post-exercise ≥20 mmHg — any one is diagnostic. Mechanism: exercise raises muscle volume by up to ~20% within non-compliant fascia; intramuscular pressure rises until interstitial pressure approaches capillary perfusion pressure, collapsing the transmural gradient and reducing capillary flow, producing reproducible exertional pain that resolves within minutes of stopping. Caveat: thresholds have imperfect accuracy and must be read alongside a characteristic history.
  30. Where exactly is Baxter's nerve entrapped, and what single anatomical fact causes it to be misdiagnosed?
    The first branch of the lateral plantar nerve is entrapped principally between the deep fascia of abductor hallucis and the medial caudal margin of quadratus plantae, with a second site anterior to the medial calcaneal tuberosity beneath the long plantar ligament. It has no cutaneous sensory territory — so entrapment causes no skin numbness, and clinicians wrongly exclude a nerve lesion on the basis of intact plantar sensation and default to plantar fasciitis. Isolated abductor digiti minimi fatty atrophy on MRI is close to pathognomonic.
  31. Explain tendinopathy at the matrix level and state why the tendon is thicker but weaker.
    Tenocytes shift to a rounded, chondrocyte-like phenotype and upregulate large hydrophilic aggregating proteoglycans (aggrecan, versican) in place of the small leucine-rich decorin that normally organises fibril spacing. These bind water; ground substance volume rises; fibrils separate and lose interfibrillar connection; load sharing degrades and strain concentrates locally, sustaining the cycle. The tendon thickens because the added volume is water and ground substance, not aligned load-bearing collagen — so cross-sectional area rises while competence per unit area falls.
  32. Give Frost's mechanostat windows and the BMU timeline, then explain why bone stress injury presents weeks after the training error.
    Mechanostat: disuse <~50–100 με; adapted ~1,000–1,500 με; modelling/mild overload ~1,500–3,000 με; pathological overload >~3,000–4,000 με; fracture ~10,000–25,000 με. BMU sequence: activation (days) → osteoclastic resorption (~2–4 weeks) → reversal (~1–2 weeks) → osteoblastic formation (~3 months) → mineralisation (months). Resorption precedes formation, so cortical porosity rises transiently and the bone is weaker than before the stimulus for several weeks. The athlete therefore feels fine during the spike week and becomes symptomatic 2–4 weeks later, during the porosity window. History-taking must interrogate the training log 2–6 weeks before onset.
